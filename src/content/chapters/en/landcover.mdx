Counting rice, stratifying a sampling frame (Chapter 5), knowing how much farmland has gone under houses: all of it starts from a <Term id="land-cover">land cover</Term> map. Since 2021 anyone has three free 10 m maps of the whole world: **ESA WorldCover**, **Google Dynamic World** and **Esri / Impact Observatory LULC**. All three come from Sentinel images, for the same year, at the same resolution. Yet over the two deltas they disagree on the things that matter most for agriculture. This chapter follows chapter 2 of the FAO (2017) handbook: the concept of land cover, agriculture in legends, and the products available.

## 3.1 Land cover and land use

Land cover is what is **seen** on the ground: grass, trees, water, roofs. <Term id="land-use">Land use</Term> is what people **do** there: grow rice, farm shrimp, live. The FAO handbook stresses that "cultivated land" is really a land use class: as cover, a rice field is dense herbaceous vegetation, and for a few weeks a year open water. Yet every land cover map has agricultural classes, because users need them. That is the root of many disagreements.

To make two maps "speak the same language", FAO built <Term id="lccs">LCCS</Term> and then LCML (standard ISO 19144-2): each class is defined by independent criteria such as life form, cover, height and flooding, not just by a name. Agricultural statistics (the World Programme for the Census of Agriculture 2020) use these definitions:

| Concept | Includes |
|---|---|
| Arable land | land under temporary crops during the year, including temporary fallow |
| Cropland | arable land + land under permanent crops (fruit trees, coffee, rubber…) |
| Agricultural land | cropland + permanent meadows and pastures |

Remote sensing works with a narrower definition, from the JECAM network: <Term id="annual-cropland">annual cropland</Term> is land of at least 0.25 ha sown and harvested at least once within 12 months. Woody perennials are excluded. In the Mekong Delta, where fruit orchards and coconut cover whole districts, the difference is large. The table summarises how the three products define the classes, from the producers' documents and the comparison by Venter et al. (2022).

| Case | WorldCover | Dynamic World | Esri |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fruit orchards, coconut | Tree cover | Trees ("plantations… citrus") | Definition: trees ("plantations"); in practice in Ben Tre: mostly built area or crops (section 3.4) |
| Flooded rice paddies | Cropland | Crops or flooded vegetation | Definition: flooded vegetation ("rice paddies"); in practice in the Mekong: mostly crops |
| Fallow fields | Grassland | Crops ("fallow plots") | Crops ("fallow plots") |
| Greenhouses | Built-up | — | — |

<Note kind="key">

Before taking the "cropland" figure of any map, read its class definitions. Perennial orchards, flooded paddies and fallow land are classed differently in each product, and in the two deltas they cover hundreds of thousands of hectares.

</Note>

In the Mekong Delta, all three maps call 32.4 % of the compared area cropland. Of the 2,414 thousand ha that at least one map calls cropland, all three agree on only 52.8 %. The most common disagreement is 'WorldCover + Esri' (11.1 % of the area), then 'Esri only' (8.9 %) and 'WorldCover only' (5.6 %); Dynamic World almost never calls a cell cropland on its own (0.4 %). In the Red River Delta all three agree on 28.3 % of the area, that is 58.2 % of the part at least one map calls cropland. Over all seven classes, each pair of maps agrees on 58.6–70.0 % of the Mekong and 66.7–84.7 % of the Red River Delta; the most similar pair in both is Dynamic World and Esri.

<Note kind="tip">

High agreement does not mean correct: two maps can be wrong in the same place. But low agreement means at least one map is wrong. 'All three' areas are safe places for training samples; areas of disagreement are where to go and check (<Term id="map-agreement">map agreement</Term>).

</Note>

In the Mekong Delta, cropland is 1,949 thousand ha on WorldCover, 1,409 on Dynamic World and 2,160 on Esri. Trees differ even more: 1,081, 523 and only 150 thousand ha. Built-up goes the other way: 175 thousand ha on WorldCover, 772 on Dynamic World and 800 on Esri, more than four times as much. Dynamic World puts 297 thousand ha into flooded vegetation, probably mostly flooded paddies; adding them brings its cropland to 1,706 thousand ha. Esri, although its definition places rice paddies in flooded vegetation, has only 17 thousand ha of that class in the Mekong: paddies sit in its crops class. In the Red River Delta, cropland is closer (658, 573 and 677 thousand ha), but built-up still differs more than twofold (245, 511 and 537 thousand ha), and so do trees (512, 280 and 219 thousand ha).

The comparison covers the part of each region outline inside the map frame: 3,926 thousand ha in the Mekong, 1,659 thousand ha in the Red River Delta (the frame leaves out most of Quang Ninh and the islands, Chapter 1).

The Ben Tre frame is orchard country: coconut and fruit trees mixed with houses and ditches. WorldCover calls 75.3 % of it trees and only 3.3 % built-up; Dynamic World 53.1 % trees and 29.3 % built-up; Esri 25.4 % trees, 43.3 % built-up and 28.7 % crops. The same land, three answers. The Nam Dinh frame holds rice fields, dyke villages, ponds and sea: the maps agree better on water (38.7–47.6 % including the sea), but cropland still ranges from 22.4 % (Dynamic World) to 32.9 % (WorldCover), and built-up from 15.2 % to 27.4 %. Red River villages are blocks of houses, gardens and ponds: whether a map lumps the whole block into 'built-up' or splits it depends on the model.

## 3.5 Do not subtract two maps to measure change

Esri provides a map every year since 2017, so it is tempting to subtract two years. The FAO handbook (section 2.4.3) warns that with accuracies of 70–85 %, the real yearly change is much smaller than each map's error rate, so the difference of two maps is mostly error. The Esri cropland series of the two deltas shows it.

<Figure
  title="Figure 3.4 — Cropland, trees, built-up and flooded vegetation on the Esri map, each year 2017–2025"
  try="How much does Red River cropland fall from 2017 to 2024? Which years jump oddly? In the Mekong, when cropland falls, which class rises to match?"
  source="Esri / Impact Observatory 10 m Annual LULC (sat-io on Earth Engine), one map per year; areas at 30 m over the whole region outline (including the part outside the map frame, so the 2021 values differ slightly from Figure 3.2); shared legend (crops = class 5, flooded vegetation = class 4)."
>
  <LandcoverChart client:visible lang="en" view="series" />
</Figure>

According to Esri, Mekong cropland fell from 2,289 thousand ha in 2017 to 2,024 thousand ha in 2024 (−11.6 %), while built-up rose from 612 to 900 thousand ha. In the Red River Delta, cropland fell from 779 to 656 thousand ha (−15.7 %), built-up from 472 to 611 thousand ha. Part of this is real: cities, industrial zones and roads take farmland in both deltas. But look at the steps: the Mekong gains 89 thousand ha in 2018, loses 135 thousand ha in 2019 and 116 thousand ha in 2020, then gains 81 thousand ha in 2025. A whole delta's cropland does not swing by a hundred thousand hectares a year. And 900 thousand ha of built-up land is nearly a quarter of the Mekong Delta, where WorldCover sees 175 thousand ha in 2021.

<Note kind="warn">

A change on a yearly map can be real change, a new model version, or a cloudier year. To measure rice land lost, detect change directly in the image time series (as Chapter 4 counts rice seasons from radar) and estimate the area with a check sample (Chapter 5), rather than subtracting two maps.

</Note>

## 3.6 Choosing a map for agriculture

<Steps items={[
  { t: 'Read the class definitions', d: 'Does cropland include orchards, flooded paddies, fallow land? Does it match the statistical concept to be measured?' },
  { t: 'Look on the ground', d: 'Open the three maps at a few familiar places (as in Figure 3.3) and compare with very high resolution images. Where do they disagree?' },
  { t: 'Use for strata, not for numbers', d: 'Global maps are good for stratifying a sampling frame and as auxiliary variables (Chapter 5), not as a replacement for the statistic.' },
  { t: 'Crop-specific maps', d: 'Rice has a very clear temporal signature: a rice map from radar time series (Chapter 4) beats any generic "cropland" class.' },
]} />

The FAO handbook (section 2.5) presents efforts dedicated to agriculture: the USGS 30 m global cropland map GFSAD30, the Sen2-Agri system producing in-season 10 m cropland masks and crop type maps, and radar rice monitoring programmes such as Asia-RiCE. For rice, the handbook notes, radar mapping has matured: rice has such a specific temporal signature that it is usually treated not as a land cover problem but as crop monitoring.

<Note kind="key">

Three maps from the same images and the same year still give three different numbers, because they define classes differently and resolve mixed landscapes differently. In the two deltas, orchards, villages and flooded paddies are the hard cases. Use global maps as auxiliary variables and as the big picture; the numbers themselves have to be estimated.

</Note>
