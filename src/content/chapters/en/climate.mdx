Rice needs water and warmth. In the Red River Delta the cold winter makes the spring crop wait until the cold spells end. In the Mekong Delta the temperature hardly changes through the year, so water sets the calendar: flood, rain and salt. This chapter measures those factors from satellites and reanalysis for both regions over 45 years.

## 7.1 Two climates

**The Red River Delta** has a tropical monsoon climate with a real cold winter. January averages only 16.4 °C, June reaches 28.8 °C: an annual range of 12.4 °C. Rain falls mostly from May to October, peaking in August (336 mm); winter is dry but often drizzly and overcast (Chapter 2).

**The Mekong Delta** is hot all year: the coolest month is 25.8 °C, the hottest (April) 28.6 °C, a range of only 2.8 °C. Its annual rain almost equals the Red River Delta's (1,705 against 1,698 mm), but the seasons are harsher: December to April gets only 9 % of the year's rain, and February averages 7 mm.

<Note kind="key">

The two deltas get almost the same annual rain, but the Red River Delta is limited by **heat** (a cold winter) and the Mekong Delta by **water** (a rainless dry season, a flooded wet season). That is why the north grows two crops following the temperature, and the south two to three crops following irrigation and the flood (Chapter 4).

</Note>

## 7.2 Rain seen from space

Rain gauges in the two deltas are sparse and their records are not open. <Term id="chirps">CHIRPS</Term> fills the gap: it estimates rain from cold-cloud duration in geostationary infrared imagery, then corrects it with gauge data, at 0.05° (about 5.5 km) since 1981 (Funk et al., 2015).

## 7.3 Air temperature and surface temperature

The two "temperatures" in this chapter measure different things.

- **Air temperature at 2 m** comes from the ERA5-Land <Term id="reanalysis">reanalysis</Term> (0.1°, since 1950). It is what a weather station measures and what the rice plant "feels" through the air.
- **MODIS <Term id="lst">land surface temperature</Term>** is the temperature of the field, canopy or roof itself, measured in thermal infrared bands when Terra passes at about 10:30 in the morning, and only under clear skies.

The gap between the two temperatures tells whether a field holds green, transpiring plants or lies bare and dry. That is the basis of the Temperature Condition Index (TCI) in Chapter 9. In the Red River Delta the winter gap reaches 6–8 °C, but part of it is bias: MODIS measures only on the rare clear days, and clear days are usually sunnier and warmer than the monthly mean.

<Depth level="advanced" title="What a reanalysis is, and when to doubt it">

ERA5-Land re-runs the ECMWF land-surface model for every hour since 1950, driven by the ERA5 atmosphere, which assimilates millions of observations from stations, radiosondes and satellites. The result is continuous, with no cloud gaps. But it is a **model product**: 0.1° (about 11 km) does not see flooded paddies or towns; the model does not know how much farmers irrigate; and as the observing system changes over the decades, long-term trends can drift. Compare ERA5-Land trends with station records where possible.

</Depth>

## 7.4 Evapotranspiration and the water balance

<Term id="evapotranspiration">Evapotranspiration</Term> is the water a field returns to the atmosphere. Comparing it with rain shows in which months crops are self-sufficient and in which they need irrigation.

According to ERA5-Land, actual evapotranspiration is about 1,100 mm a year in the Red River Delta and 1,265 mm in the Mekong Delta. In the Mekong Delta rain stays below potential evapotranspiration from November to May; the largest deficit is in March, about 280 mm. That is when the winter–spring crop heads and ripens, living on river water just as the salt moves in (Chapter 10).

<Note kind="warn">

ERA5-Land computes potential evaporation as if the surface were always wet, so its absolute values (about 2,000–2,650 mm a year) are higher than the FAO-56 reference evapotranspiration ET₀. Read only **which season is short of water**, not the size of the deficit.

</Note>

## 7.5 El Niño and the dry seasons of 2016, 2020 and 2024

<Term id="enso">ENSO</Term> is the largest mode of tropical climate variability. NOAA's <Term id="oni">ONI</Term> measures it: December–February of the 2015–16 dry season was +2.50 (very strong El Niño), 2019–20 +0.71 (weak), 2023–24 +1.84 (strong).

The three dry seasons tell three different stories in the Mekong Delta:

- **2015–16**: rain 34 % short, Feb–Apr 0.9 °C warmer. Drought came with record salinity (Chapter 10).
- **2019–20**: rain 38 % short, about 70 % of the deficit in December–January; Feb–Apr was only slightly dry (SPI-3 −0.14, Chapter 9). Local rain does not explain that year's salinity (Chapter 10).
- **2023–24**: rain 60 % short, level with the driest years since 1981 (1987, 2005); Feb–Apr the hottest since 1981 (+1.3 °C).

<Note kind="warn">

In August–October 2026 the ONI reached +2.46, the highest since the 2015–16 season (NOAA CPC, data downloaded 17 November 2026). Since 1950 only three seasons had a December–February ONI above +2 (1982–83, 1997–98, 2015–16); all three times the Mekong dry season was 34–59 % short of rain. ENSO does not decide everything, but it is reason to watch the 2026–27 dry season closely with the indices of Chapters 9 and 10.

</Note>

According to ERA5-Land, the annual temperature rose by about 0.19 °C per decade in the Red River Delta and 0.13 °C in the Mekong Delta over 1981–2025. The hottest year was 2019 in the Red River Delta and 2024 in the Mekong Delta. The trend uses the Theil–Sen method (median slope between all pairs of years), so one unusually hot El Niño year does not pull the line the way least squares would.

<Note kind="key">

A trend from a reanalysis is an estimate, not a measurement. Before using it for decisions, compare it with the nearest weather station and state the source, period and method.

</Note>
