The coast is where land, fresh water, salt water and people meet. Here rice gives way to shrimp, mangroves are cut and replanted, and the shoreline itself moves. Landsat has imaged both deltas since the late 1980s, long enough to see those changes.

## 12.1 Two kinds of coast

The Mekong Delta coast is mostly a **mud coast**: fine Mekong sediment carried by longshore currents builds mud flats, and mangroves advance over them. When sediment runs short, a mud coast retreats fast. Anthony et al. (2015) measured on satellite images that over 2003–2012 more than 50 % of the more than 600 km of Mekong Delta coast was eroding; along the 180 km of East Sea mud coast, nearly 90 % was retreating, at a mean rate that rose from about 6.4 m/yr to over 12.5 m/yr. The authors link this to human activities: upstream dams and river-bed sand mining trap the sediment, leaving the coast in <Term id="sediment-starvation">sediment starvation</Term>.

The Red River Delta coast at Nam Dinh shows both faces. The Ba Lat mouth of the Red River still builds out: over 1998–2008, new land advanced on average about 36 m/yr along 64.3 km of coast, and up to 93 m/yr at Con Ngan – Con Lu (Nguyen Van Thao et al., 2013). Further southwest, the Hai Hau coast was eroding: 9 m/yr on average over 1975–1989, up to 30 m/yr in places (Pham Quang Son and Nguyen Duc Anh, 2016).

## 12.2 Drawing the shoreline from Landsat

The land–water boundary is sharpest in the shortwave infrared, where water absorbs almost all light. The MNDWI water index (Chapter 8) is positive over water and negative over land and vegetation. But every image is taken at some tide stage. Landsat passes at about 10 a.m., at a different tide each time, so on a wide <Term id="tidal-flat">tidal mud flat</Term> the same pixel is sometimes water, sometimes land.

The fix used here: take **every** clear image over five years and count the share of looks in which a pixel is water. A pixel that is water in at least half its looks counts as water. That boundary is a median-tide <Term id="shoreline">shoreline</Term>. Comparing the periods 1988–1992 and 2021–2025 gives a land–water change map.

<Note kind="warn">

This map knows only **land** and **water**, not why. "Land to water" may be an eroded shore, a newly dug pond or a field flooded longer; "water to land" may be new land, a filled pond or young mangroves closing over a mud flat. Explaining it is the reader's job, from shape, position and field knowledge. The two periods are not balanced either: 1988–92 has only Landsat 5, with fewer images, and both pool all months, so fields flooded long in the rainy season can count as water. Figure 12.5 uses dry-season images only to check the ponds again.

</Note>

## 12.3 Land gained and lost, stretch by stretch

To get numbers, add up the land area each year in a band 1.5 km either side of the old shoreline (or in a frame around a river mouth). Single years swing by hundreds of hectares with the tide and the number of images, so take the **Theil–Sen trend** (Chapter 7) over all well-observed years instead of comparing two years. Dividing the rate of change in area by the length of the stretch gives the mean rate of shoreline shift:

Three kinds of coast stand out:

- **Steady retreat**: the Ca Mau East Sea coast lost 198 ha a year, about 6,500 ha in 33 years, a shoreline retreat of 29.7 m/yr, close to the 33.2 m/yr of Tran Thi et al. (2014). Behind this stretch the mangrove belt is intact (Figure 12.3), so nearly all the land lost is at the sea edge, not ponds dug within the 1.5 km band.
- **Building out**: the Gulf of Thailand side of the Cape gained 144 ha a year (about 4,700 ha in 33 years), the Ba Lat mouth 68 ha a year (about 2,200 ha). Tran Thi et al. (2014) measured 40.7 m/yr of accretion on the Gulf of Thailand side of the Cape.
- **No clear trend**: the 24.6 km Hai Hau band changed by only +10 ha/yr (+4 m/yr), while the swing between consecutive years is typically about 130 ha, and the band also holds inland ponds and salt pans. At this resolution the satellite does not see Hai Hau retreating over 1988–2025. Sea dikes and revetments holding the line may be one reason, but this chapter does not test that; strong erosion has been measured on short stretches such as Hai Ly (−22.6 m/yr over 1998–2008, Nguyen Van Thao et al., 2013).

<Note kind="key">

One image cannot measure a shoreline on a tidal mud coast; many years of images can. The 29.7 m/yr from 38 years of Landsat is close to an independent study that used other data and another period, but it gives only the **mean over the whole stretch**. To find the fastest-retreating spots, measure along transects normal to the coast, as the studies cited here do.

</Note>

## 12.4 Mangroves

In the Ca Mau Cape frame, 7,700 ha of the 1996 mangroves were gone by 2020 and 2,300 ha are new. The losses form a continuous band along the East Sea coast, right where the shoreline retreats (Figure 12.1): there the forest was not cut, the ground under it was washed away. New mangroves grow on the new land west of the Cape.

Mekong Delta mangroves in GMW fell by 7.2 %, from 96,100 ha (1996) to 89,200 ha (2020), mostly before 2010. The GMW epochs are not all built from the same radar data, so part of the difference between 1996 and 2007 may come from the data rather than real change. ## 12.5 Shrimp ponds and the switch from rice to shrimp

Many salt-affected rice areas of the Ca Mau peninsula have switched to shrimp farming, or to rice in the rainy season rotated with shrimp in the dry season (Chapter 10). On dry-season images an <Term id="aquaculture-pond">aquaculture pond</Term> is water almost all season, while a winter–spring rice field is flooded only briefly during land preparation. Counting the share of 30 m cells under water in at least three quarters of the January–April looks separates shrimp ponds (with rivers and canals) from rice fields.

Across the Mekong Delta, standing dry-season water grew from 226,100 ha (1993–97) to 398,700 ha (2003–07), then eased to 368,200 ha (2021–25). Nearly all the growth is on the Ca Mau peninsula: Ca Mau added 108,500 ha and Bac Lieu 42,200 ha in the first ten years alone. Both then fell back a little (Ca Mau to 125,400 ha, Bac Lieu to 41,900 ha), while Kien Giang kept growing (15,900 to 43,900 ha). The images cannot say why they fell: ponds switching to rice–shrimp rotation (rice in the field at the end of the dry season), intensive ponds drained between crops, or ponds abandoned. In inland provinces such as An Giang and Can Tho the figure barely changed in thirty years.

<Note kind="key">

The satellite shows clearly **when and where** coastal rice land turned into ponds: a big wave of conversion in Ca Mau and Bac Lieu between the mid-1990s and the mid-2000s. It does not show what is raised in the ponds, how, or whether it pays. For that, the map has to be joined with fisheries statistics and farm surveys.

</Note>

<Depth level="advanced" title="Telling shrimp ponds from rivers and flooded fields">

The 75 % threshold on January–April looks still mixes three things: rivers and big canals (water all year, unchanged between periods), aquaculture ponds (water all year, new) and long-flooded fields (rare in the dry season). Further separation: (1) subtract the JRC permanent water (Chapter 8); (2) shape: ponds are embanked rectangles, clear at 10 m on Sentinel-2; (3) water calendar: rice–shrimp ponds dry out in the rainy season when rice is grown, while intensive shrimp ponds stay flooded all year. A trustworthy aquaculture map still needs checking against provincial statistics.

</Depth>
