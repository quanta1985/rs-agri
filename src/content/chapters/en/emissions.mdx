Flooded paddies give off methane (CH₄). After each harvest, the straw is often burned where it lies, sending up smoke and gases. Both are farm emissions. Satellites see part of them: a fire hot enough and large enough, burning when the satellite passes; and the amount of gas in the whole column of air above a cell a few kilometres wide. The rest has to be calculated from rice area, irrigation practice and emission factors.

## 13.1 What farming emits, and what satellites see

Viet Nam's Third Biennial Update Report (BUR3) to the UN climate convention puts rice cultivation in 2016 at 1,774.75 thousand tonnes of CH₄, or 49.7 million tonnes of CO₂ equivalent (with the IPCC AR5 <Term id="gwp">global warming potential</Term> of 28). That is 15.7 % of the country's net emissions and nearly 60 % of agricultural emissions (excluding forest land). Rice cultivation is the second key category in the national <Term id="ghg-inventory">greenhouse gas inventory</Term>.

CH₄ forms when bacteria break down organic matter in flooded, oxygen-poor soil. The longer a field stays flooded, and the more straw is ploughed in just before planting, the more it emits. Straw has another fate too: fire. The Mekong Delta produced about 29.16 million tonnes of dry straw in 2019, out of 53.3 million tonnes for the whole country (Cuong et al., 2021). In the Mekong Delta, 50–60 % of straw is burned in the field (Nguyen-Van-Hung et al., 2019, citing an IRRI survey from 2014).

| Source | What satellites see | What they miss |
|---|---|---|
| Straw burning | Hot spots (MODIS, VIIRS); smoke gases NO₂ and CO in the air column (Sentinel-5P) | Small fires, short fires, fires under cloud or between overpasses |
| Paddy CH₄ | Column-average CH₄ in 5.5 × 7 km cells (Sentinel-5P) | Emissions of individual fields; nearly every cloudy day |
| Indirect | Rice area and calendar, flooded or dry fields (Chapters 4, 8) | Fertiliser, variety, actual irrigation practice |
| N₂O from nitrogen fertiliser | Nothing | All of it |

<Note kind="key">

Satellites do not measure emissions. They measure **signs**: a hot spot, a column of gas denser than its surroundings, a flooded field. Turning signs into tonnes of CH₄ or of dust takes models and emission factors. Remote sensing is strongest here as a source of **activity data**: rice area, number of seasons, flooding calendar, burned area.

</Note>

## 13.2 Catching fires from space

A fire burning at several hundred degrees glows strongly in the mid-infrared, around 4 µm. Even a small fire makes a 1 km MODIS pixel clearly warmer in that band than in the 11 µm thermal band and than its neighbours. The <Term id="active-fire">active fire</Term> algorithm looks for that contrast. According to the MODIS active fire user's guide (Giglio et al., 2021), MODIS routinely detects fires of about 1,000 m², fires ten times smaller under very good conditions, and flaming fires of only about 50 m² under ideal ones.

"Can detect" is not "detects all". Against ASTER images taken at the same time at 30 m, MODIS misses 86.2 % of fires, mostly small ones; it almost never misses large fires (Giglio et al., 2016). Terra passes at about 10:30 and 22:30, Aqua at about 13:30 and 01:30. A pile of straw lit at 16:00 that burns for an hour, or one burning under cloud, never enters the record. Lasko et al. (2017) blame cloud, small fires, short fires and burning after the overpass for global inventories underestimating straw burning in Viet Nam. The 375 m VIIRS sensor sees more fire pixels: 3 times more by day and 25 times more by night than the 750 m product of the same sensor (Schroeder et al., 2014).

Figure 13.1 counts the days each year on which a 1 km cell had at least one MODIS fire detection, averaged over five-year periods. In 2001–2002 only Terra was flying (Aqua data begin in July 2002), so the numbers in this chapter start in 2003.

In 2003–2025, 75 % of the fire days in the Mekong Delta were on cropland (cells at least half cropland in ESA WorldCover 2021), 8 % on forest and wetland and 17 % on other land. The Red River Delta is the reverse: only 10 % on cropland, 57 % on forest and wetland, 33 % on other land. The days with the most fires that we checked (December 2024, January 2025) cluster in the Quang Ninh hills, which lie inside the Red River statistical region.

<Note kind="warn">

A rice field burns once or twice a season. Cells that "burn" more than 10 days a year, such as the cell at 106.13° E, 21.24° N (Red River Delta, 13.8 days/yr in 2021–2025) or the one at 106.32° E, 10.47° N (Mekong Delta, 14.2 days/yr), are most likely not straw fires but heat sources that recur in one place, such as factories or kilns. The standard MODIS product labels hot spots seen on 16 or more days in a year, or in urban areas, as "other static land source" (Giglio et al., 2021). The near-real-time FIRMS data used here carry no such label, so they must be removed by hand before counting "straw burning".

</Note>

## 13.3 The straw-burning calendar

In the Mekong Delta, winter–spring rice is sown after the flood recedes, in November–December (Chapter 4), and harvested about 100 days later. Cropland fires follow that calendar: March has on average 146 fire days per 1,000 1 km cells, February 35, April 71. February–April hold 78 % of the year's fire days. Summer–autumn and autumn–winter rice are harvested in the rainy season, when straw is wet and skies are cloudy, and hardly any fires show up.

The Red River Delta is different. Its cropland has only 54 fire days per 1,000 cells a year, against 324 in the Mekong Delta. They spread evenly from May to September (81 % of fire days) and drop to almost nothing in January–March. January–March in the north is also when the fewest optical images are usable (Chapter 2): cloud and drizzle hide both fire and ground. The Red River fire record therefore reflects the cloud calendar as much as the burning calendar, and cannot be read as the true burning season.

In the Mekong Delta, cropland fire days rise and fall with the weather, from 96 per 1,000 cells (2006) to 586 (2020). In the Red River Delta they climb steadily: from 8 per 1,000 cells a year on average in 2003–2007 to 92 in 2021–2025. The satellite cannot say why. Farmers may be burning more, or the fires may be larger or closer to overpass time. Finding out takes fieldwork.

<Note kind="key">

The Mekong fire record reads as a winter–spring harvest calendar, and its size follows the dryness of the dry season. That much the satellite gives reliably. Burned area, tonnes of straw and tonnes of smoke it does not: MODIS misses most small fires, so fire counts are a **relative index** for comparing years and regions.

</Note>

## 13.4 Smoke, NO₂ and CO

Sentinel-5P carries the TROPOMI instrument, covers nearly the whole Earth every day and passes at about 13:30 local time (ESA). It measures sunlight reflected through the whole atmosphere and works out the amount of each gas above each square metre of ground: nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), carbon monoxide (CO), methane (CH₄) and others. These are <Term id="column-amount">column amounts</Term>, not the ground-level concentration that people breathe.

NO₂ comes mainly from burning fuel: traffic, power plants, industry. It lasts only hours in the air, so an NO₂ map shows where the burning happens. Averaged over the months of 2019–2025 across the whole region, tropospheric NO₂ over the Red River Delta was 64.8 µmol/m², more than three times the Mekong Delta (20.2 µmol/m²). The highest Red River cell (185 µmol/m²) lies north of central Hai Phong; Ha Noi is about 85 µmol/m². In the Mekong Delta the highest cell (47.6 µmol/m²) lies on the eastern edge, next to Ho Chi Minh City. No rice land stands out.

CO lives far longer than NO₂, long enough for the wind to carry it a long way. Over the Mekong Delta, column CO is highest in January–April (about 38–39 mmol/m²) and lowest in July (24.5), at the time of local straw burning. Over the Red River Delta, CO peaks in March–April (66.0 and 63.6 mmol/m²), twice the July value, while fires on Red River farmland are then very few (Figure 13.2); fires on forest land peak in January–May. A region's CO column holds both CO made there and CO carried in by the wind, and these data cannot separate the two.

Sentinel-5P does not measure fine dust from straw fires directly. Lasko and Vadrevu (2018) estimate that rice residue burning in Viet Nam released about 150 thousand tonnes of PM2.5 in 2015, 14–18 % of the country's PM2.5 emissions. That estimate rests on rice area, an assumed burned fraction and emission factors, not on fire counts, which are far too low.

## 13.5 Paddy CH₄: seen from above, calculated from below

TROPOMI measures <Term id="xch4">XCH₄</Term>, the column-average dry-air mixing ratio of CH₄, in ppb (parts per billion). A measurement cell is 5.5 × 7 km (since August 2019). A retrieval is kept only when the sky is almost cloud-free, the surface bright enough and aerosol low (Lorente et al., 2021). Over cloudy tropics and dark flooded fields, that is rare.

Averaged over 2019–2025, XCH₄ over the Red River Delta was 1,951 ppb, 20.9 ppb above the band at the same latitude (100–112° E) outside the delta. The Mekong Delta was 12.7 ppb above its band. The comparison band mixes hills, forest, cities and surfaces of different brightness, so these differences are not a paddy signal as such. The number of looks also differs widely: over seven years a 1.1 km cell has a median of 49 valid looks in the Red River Delta, only 17 in the Mekong Delta (about 6 on the western rice land), against about 170 in north-eastern Thailand. The highest 10 km cells in the frame (about 1,970 ppb) lie in the Pearl River Delta (China) and around Ha Noi.

Over the Red River Delta, XCH₄ in September–November (1,948–1,957 ppb, mean of the years) is above January–July (1,905–1,918 ppb; July has data in only 4 years). The peak falls during and just after the flooded summer rice season (July–October). Wójcik-Gront et al. (2025) also find a seasonal swing of about 30 ppb, peaking in the fourth quarter, over high-emission regions including the Mekong Delta, against about 17 ppb in low-emission regions. Part of that seasonal cycle belongs to the whole atmosphere, not to the paddies alone. The series also climbs from year to year: over the Red River Delta from 1,861–1,893 ppb in January–April 2019 to 1,944–1,970 ppb in October–December 2025, so compare months within the same year. Over the Mekong Delta there is not one September value in seven years. The satellite is absent exactly when the fields are most flooded.

That is why national inventories do not measure CH₄ from space but calculate it from below, with the IPCC method:

<Formula label="CH₄ from one rice season (IPCC 2019, Tier 1)" tex="\mathrm{CH_4} = EF_c \times SF_w \times SF_p \times SF_o \times t \times A" where="EFc: baseline factor, kg CH₄/ha/day (continuously flooded, no organic amendment; South-East Asia 1.22); SFw: water regime in the season (continuous flooding 1; single drainage 0.71; multiple drainage, including alternate wetting and drying, 0.55); SFp: water regime before the season (dry under 180 days 1; flooded over 30 days 2.41); SFo = (1 + Σ rate × CFOA)^0.59: organic amendments (straw incorporated under 30 days before planting CFOA = 1; over 30 days 0.19; burned or surface straw not counted); t: season length in days (South-East Asia default 102); A: area (ha)" />

With the South-East Asia defaults, one hectare of continuously flooded rice over 102 days emits 124.4 kg CH₄, or 3.36 t CO₂ equivalent (with the IPCC AR6 factor of 27). Times the 1,487.7 thousand ha of winter–spring rice in the Mekong Delta in 2024 (General Statistics Office, Chapter 4), that is about 185 thousand tonnes of CH₄ for one season, assuming every field is continuously flooded with no straw ploughed in. Ploughing in 3 t of dry straw per hectare just before planting multiplies it by 2.27; ploughing it in more than 30 days earlier, by only 1.30.

<Depth level="advanced" title="Tier 1, Tier 2 and factors measured in Viet Nam">

Tier 1 uses global or regional default factors; Tier 2 replaces them with factors measured in the country. Vo et al. (2020) pooled chamber measurements from Viet Nam and give baseline factors from 1.72 to 3.89 kg CH₄/ha/day depending on region and season of the year (early, mid and late year), all above the IPCC South-East Asia default of 1.22. Arai (2022) measured 15 seasons in a row in a triple-cropped field in the Mekong Delta and found continuous-flooding emissions 1.1–2.7 times the factors used in Viet Nam. Two lessons follow: the defaults may underestimate emissions here, and any paddy CH₄ estimate carries a large error. Remote sensing shrinks the error in **area and calendar**, not in the factors.

</Depth>

## 13.6 Alternate wetting and drying, and low-emission rice

<Term id="awd">Alternate wetting and drying</Term> (AWD) lets the field dry until the water falls below the soil surface before it is flooded again, several times a season. The soil gets air, and the bacteria that make CH₄ slow down. IPCC puts AWD under "multiple drainage", factor 0.55: 45 % less than continuous flooding. Trials in Viet Nam point the same way: over 5 years (15 seasons) in a triple-cropped field in the Mekong Delta, AWD cut annual CH₄ by 51 % and raised yield by 9 % (Arai, 2022). In the north, early plus mid-season drainage cut CH₄ by 67 % with good water management and by 43 % with poor management (Tariq et al., 2017).

Targets: 180 thousand ha by 2025 and one million ha by 2030; greenhouse gas emissions more than 10 % below conventional rice; 20 % less irrigation water; straw collected from the fields for reuse, 70 % by 2025 and 100 % by 2030; and every hectare under at least one sustainable practice such as AWD.

<Note kind="key">

The CH₄ saved by low-emission rice will be **calculated**, not **measured** from space. Satellites make the calculated number more credible by confirming area, crop calendar, drainage and straw burning. That is also where the FAO (2017) handbook places remote sensing in agricultural statistics: it supplies activity data and sampling frames, while the final figures still need measurements on the ground.

</Note>
