Water sets the farming calendar in both deltas. In the Mekong delta the yearly flood brings sediment and fish, but also limits how many rice crops a field can carry. In the Red River Delta the river runs between dykes, and the main worry is fields waterlogged by typhoon rain. Satellites show where water is and when. They do not directly tell whether a crop was harmed: making that difference clear is the aim of this chapter.

This chapter does not re-teach radar or spectral indices. For a refresher, see the <a href="https://quanta1985.github.io/rs-knowledge/en/ch11-sar/">SAR radar chapter</a> and the <a href="https://quanta1985.github.io/rs-knowledge/en/ch06-spectral/">spectral indices chapter</a> of the *Remote Sensing & GIS* handbook.

## 8.1 Two deltas, two water regimes

The **Mekong delta** sits at the end of the Mekong basin. In the wet season, water from upstream rises from about July, spills into the low Plain of Reeds (Đồng Tháp Mười) and Long Xuyen Quadrangle, then drains until December. This yearly cycle is the <Term id="flood-pulse">flood pulse</Term>. The flood is slow and rises gradually. It brings sediment, flushes acidity, carries fish onto the fields, and decides how many rice crops each field can grow.

The **Red River Delta** is different. Upstream reservoirs (Hòa Bình, Sơn La, Lai Châu) regulate the Red River, and the fields lie behind a dyke system. River water outside the dykes only covers the floodplain. Inside the dykes the worry is <Term id="waterlogging">waterlogging</Term>: heavy typhoon rain falls faster than the drainage pumps can remove it.

Figure 8.1 compares the two deltas with <Term id="jrc-gsw">JRC Global Surface Water</Term> (Pekel et al., 2016), built from every Landsat scene since 1984. The first layer is <Term id="water-occurrence">water occurrence</Term> over 1984–2021. The second is the <Term id="water-seasonality">number of months with water</Term> in 2021.

Multiplying occurrence by cell area, "average" surface water covers **15.5 %** of the Mekong delta (615 thousand ha) and **7.4 %** of the Red River Delta (125 thousand ha). The big difference is seasonal water: in 2021, **1.35 million ha** of the Mekong delta held water for 1 to under 10 months, against 180 thousand ha in the Red River Delta.

<Note kind="key">

The Mekong delta has a seasonal flood spread across its fields; the Red River Delta has water concentrated in rivers, floodplains and ponds, with the fields behind dykes. So the remote-sensing question differs: in the Mekong delta it is "how big is this year's flood", in the Red River Delta "which fields went under water after this storm".

</Note>

<Depth level="advanced" title="Reading the JRC layers at 250 m">

JRC classifies every 30 m Landsat scene as water / not water and counts. Occurrence is the share of cloud-free observations that saw water; seasonality is the number of months in the year with at least one water observation. Here each 250 m cell is the **mean** of about 70 Landsat pixels. A cell value of 3 months can be a whole cell flooded for 3 months, or a quarter of the cell covered by a pond all year. Telling them apart needs the 30 m data.

2021 is the last year of version 1.4. Landsat misses many wet-season months under cloud, so months with water can be undercounted where no clear image exists.

</Depth>

## 8.2 Detecting water: MNDWI and radar

There are two common ways. Optical images use the <Term id="mndwi">MNDWI</Term> index (Xu, 2006): water reflects a little green light but absorbs nearly all shortwave infrared (SWIR), so water has a positive MNDWI while soil and plants are usually negative.

<Formula label="MNDWI water index (Sentinel-2)" tex="\mathrm{MNDWI} = \frac{B_3 - B_{11}}{B_3 + B_{11}}" where="
B_3 :: green reflectance (560 nm, 10 m)
B_{11} :: shortwave-infrared reflectance (1610 nm, 20 m)
" />

Radar relies on <Term id="specular-reflection">specular reflection</Term>: calm water sends the wave away from the satellite, so water is very dark. Both need a <Term id="water-threshold">water threshold</Term>. The most common automatic one is the <Term id="otsu">Otsu threshold</Term>: it picks the value that splits the histogram into the two most different groups.

The An Giang frame (10 × 10 km around Thoại Sơn – Tri Tôn) has a cloud-free Sentinel-2 scene right in the flood season, 26 October 2024, and a Sentinel-1 image of the same day. That is a rare chance to compare both methods on the same ground.

The two sensors on the same day differ by only about 2 % in area. Pixel by pixel, the two maps agree on **85.1 %** of the pixels (49.0 % water in both). 6.9 % is water only for MNDWI, 8.0 % only for radar. Disagreement sits mostly on field edges, narrow canals (a 20 m SWIR pixel is four times larger), flooded young rice and wind-roughened water.

On 12 Mar 2024 the frame holds almost no surface water (only 74 ha below −18 dB), yet Otsu still splits the histogram at −10.25 dB and calls **49 %** of the frame "water". Otsu always returns a threshold, even when the scene has only one class.

<Note kind="key">

MNDWI and radar give very similar water areas when the scene is wet and clear. Radar wins under cloud (most of the wet season); optical images are easier to read at field edges and in flooded vegetation. An Otsu threshold is only trustworthy when the histogram has two clear peaks: look at the histogram before trusting the number.

</Note>

<Depth level="advanced" title="Why not one fixed threshold for every image">

The radar water threshold changes with incidence angle, wind and water type. A shallow flooded paddy with young rice sticking out can be brighter than a river, because the wave bounces between stems and water. Flooded vegetation (melaleuca forest, tall rice) can be **brighter** than dry soil through double bounce, so it does not show as water at all. Automatic chains such as Twele et al. (2016) split the image into tiles, compute Otsu only on tiles with a two-peaked histogram, then refine with terrain. Section 8.3 uses one fixed threshold of −18 dB for the whole delta because the years must be comparable, accepting errors at edges.

</Depth>

<Note kind="key">

"Surface water" here includes rivers, canals, shrimp ponds, paddies flooded for planting and floodwater on the fields. It tells whether a flood is big or small compared with other years; it is **not** a damage area: most Mekong floodwater lies on harvested fields, just as farmers expect.

</Note>

<Depth level="advanced" title="Three processing choices and their consequences">

The **monthly median** removes single windy images (rough, brighter water) but loses floods shorter than about half a month. The **−18 dB threshold** is stricter than the Otsu value of the An Giang frame (−15.5 dB), to avoid calling wet soil water; in exchange, flooded paddies with young rice are undercounted. The **100 m resolution** lets Earth Engine process the whole delta in minutes; narrow canals are lost. From 2022 to March 2025 only Sentinel-1A flew (1B failed at the end of 2021), so each month has fewer images and a noisier median. Comparing years is still valid because every year uses the same procedure.

</Depth>

The year-to-year range is large: the highest year is more than 1.5 times the lowest. In Long An the peak fell from 125 thousand ha (2018) to 55 thousand ha (2024). An Giang ranged from 34 thousand ha (2020) to 81 thousand ha (2018). Ten years are far too short to speak of a trend, but long-term studies point to two human-made changes:

- **High dykes.** After the big flood of 2000, provinces of the Long Xuyen Quadrangle and the Plain of Reeds built many <Term id="high-dyke">high dykes</Term> that keep fields dry all year for an autumn–winter crop, replacing <Term id="low-dyke">low dykes</Term> that only hold back the early flood. Triet et al. (2017) found clear increases in peak water level and flood duration downstream of the high-dyke areas from 2000/2001. Their hydraulic model shows that high dykes alone raised the flood peak in the central delta by 9–13 cm and lengthened it by about 15 days; tides had an even larger effect (19–32 cm).
- **Upstream dams.** Binh et al. (2020) argue that in 2009–2015 the dams with large reservoirs in the basin together reduced the flood pulse and raised dry-season flow along the Mekong, more than climate change did.

Kondolf et al. (2022) name dykes as one of four causes of a "drowning" delta (with dams, sand mining and groundwater pumping): dykes stop sediment-rich floodwater from spreading over the fields and building land. They propose letting floodwater back onto the fields where possible.

## 8.5 Typhoon Yagi and flooding in the Red River Delta

Typhoon Yagi made landfall over Quảng Ninh – Hải Phòng on 7 September 2024. Heavy rain afterwards raised the Red and Thái Bình rivers and waterlogged many fields. Clouds covered the region almost the whole time. Sentinel-1 has two images on the same orbit 91 (descending) covering most of the delta: **31 August** (before) and **12 September** (five days after landfall), exactly one 12-day cycle apart.

The method is <Term id="change-detection">change detection</Term>: new water = pixels with VV < −18 dB on 12 September that were not water on 31 August. Both images share the same geometry, so every difference comes from the ground, not from the viewing angle.

The orbit-91 pair covers 95.9 % of the Red River Delta region (11 provinces). Over that area radar sees **51,501 ha** of new water, of which **23,430 ha** (45.5 %) lies on cropland according to ESA WorldCover. The rest lies on other WorldCover classes (grassland, wetland, bare ground…).

<Note kind="key">

Two radar images from the same orbit, one cycle apart, give a map of new water after a storm regardless of clouds. But the map only shows water present **when the satellite passed**: flooding before or after that moment, and damage without standing water, do not show up.

</Note>

<Depth level="advanced" title="Threshold or change detection?">

In the <a href="https://quanta1985.github.io/rs-knowledge/en/ch11-sar/">SAR radar chapter of the Remote Sensing & GIS handbook</a> (section 16.5), the same image pair over a Hanoi frame shows that "threshold on the after image" and "VV drop of more than 3 dB" give different results. Here a simple rule (water after, not water before) is used for the whole delta. The new water in the 250 m cells of Figure 8.7 is a share; summing the rise in water share over the region gives about 49 thousand ha, close to the 51,501 ha computed at 10 m.

</Depth>

Why is the water map about ten times smaller than the damage figures?

1. **Damage without water.** <Term id="storm-lodging">Lodging</Term> by wind, and empty grains from rain at flowering: there is no standing water for a satellite to see.
2. **Timing.** The 12 September image is five days after landfall. Short waterlogging was drained before then; places flooded later (late river floods) only show on the 24 September image.
3. **Canopy over water.** On 6 Sep 2024 MARD warned that about 1 million ha of rice in the north were at the flowering stage (Vietnam Agriculture newspaper, 6 Sep 2024), when the canopy is densest. C-band radar mostly "sees" the canopy; shallow water underneath can make the image **brighter** (double bounce between stems and water), not darker. This is the biggest limit of a VV threshold on growing rice.
4. **Scope.** The statistics include the northern midland and mountain provinces (the 8 September report also North Central); the map only covers the 11 delta provinces.

A post-storm water map is fast, objective evidence of **where there was water** on one day. A damage figure needs a rice map, an image series over several dates and field checks as well. Reporting a satellite number without saying what it measures is the fastest way to lose users' trust.
