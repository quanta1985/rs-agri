## 10.1 How salt gets into the rivers

Twice a day the East Sea tide pushes seawater into the Mekong (Cuu Long) estuaries, and the river pushes back. In the flood season the river is strong and the salt stays near the mouths. In the dry season (December to May) the <Term id="dry-season-discharge">dry-season discharge</Term> falls, so the tide carries saltwater farther inland. This is <Term id="salinity-intrusion">salinity intrusion</Term>. It happens every year; what changes is **how far, how early and for how long**.

Four factors push the salt limit farther inland:

1. **Little water from upstream.** The 2015 flood was the smallest in 90 years at Tan Chau, with about 220 billion m³ of flood-season water, 60 % of an average flood year. From December 2015 to March 2016 the flow at Tan Chau and Chau Doc was only 57–85 % of the long-term mean (Nguyen Ngoc Anh 2017).
2. **The <Term id="tidal-regime">tide</Term>.** Salinity reaches farthest during spring tides (around full and new moon), so salinity bulletins are usually issued per spring-tide period.
3. **<Term id="sea-level-rise">Sea-level rise</Term> and subsidence.** A higher sea relative to the land strengthens the tide in the rivers. In the Mekong Delta the land sinks by about 1–4 cm a year, mainly from groundwater pumping (Erban et al. 2014, as reviewed by Park et al. 2021), many times faster than the sea rises (see Chapter 11).
4. **<Term id="riverbed-lowering">Riverbed lowering</Term>.** Upstream dams trap sediment and sand mining removes the riverbed. Sand extraction in the delta rose from about 7.75 million m³ a year (2012) to 29.3 million m³ a year (2018) (as compiled by Park et al. 2021). Deeper channels let the tide and the salt travel farther (Eslami et al. 2019).

<Note kind="key">

Salinity is a tug-of-war between the river and the tide. Salt goes farther when the dry-season flow is small, at spring tide, and when the channel is deeper because of sand mining, sediment starvation or relative sea-level rise. Figure 10.1 only shows the direction of each effect; it cannot forecast kilometres.

</Note>

<Depth level="advanced" title="Why theory says −1/3 but measurements differ">

In a partially mixed estuary the denser saltwater creeps in along the bed while fresh water flows out on top. Classic exchange-flow theory makes the intrusion length scale with Q^(−1/3) and grow strongly with channel depth (MacCready & Geyer 2010). Real estuaries deviate because mixing changes between spring and neap tides, because of their shape, and because the salt field needs time to adjust to a new discharge. In San Francisco Bay the measured exponent is only about −1/7 (Monismith et al. 2002). The Mekong mouths have strong tides and many branches, so operational salinity forecasts use one-dimensional hydraulic models of the whole river network, calibrated with gauge data.

</Depth>

## 10.2 Three major events: 2015–16, 2019–20, 2023–24

Salinity is measured at river gauges, not from satellites. Water agencies report the <Term id="salinity-4gl">4 g/L isohaline</Term>: the distance from the river mouth to the farthest point with 4 g/L salinity, roughly where rice is clearly harmed. The quick way to measure salinity is <Term id="ec-salinity">electrical conductivity (EC)</Term>.

The three dry seasons tell three different stories:

- **2015–16** was the historic event. Salt arrived in early November 2015, about 1.5 months early. At the end-of-March 2016 peak it reached 135 km on the Vam Co Tay, 78 km on the Ham Luong, 81 km on the Co Chien and 70 km on the Hau (Bassac). Drought hit all 13 provinces and salinity the nine coastal ones (Nguyen Ngoc Anh 2017).
- **2019–20** started almost a month earlier than 2015–16 and lasted 2–2.5 times as long. The area inside the 4 g/L line was 1.68 million ha, 50,376 ha more than in 2016 (Directorate of Water Resources, 20 Jun 2020).
- **2023–24** arrived at the end of December 2023, about a month earlier than average. On 8–14 Feb 2024 the 4 g/L line at the Mekong mouths reached 40–50 km, about 5–10 km less than the same period of 2016 and 8–12 km less than 2020 (Department of Water Resources, 23 Feb 2024).

<Note kind="key">

Salinity is measured at gauges, not from satellites. Comparing events means comparing the same threshold (4 g/L), the same river and the same kind of number: season peak with season peak. In 2020 salt went as far as in 2016, yet only about a quarter as much rice was damaged, thanks to forecasts and crop calendars.

</Note>

<Depth level="advanced" title="Why sources disagree">

For the same 2015–16 season, Nguyen Ngoc Anh (2017) gives 238,276 ha of damaged rice from MARD, while a 2020 MARD report gives 405,000 ha. For intrusion length, Park et al. (2021) write that salt penetrated "up to 93 km" in 2016, while Nguyen Ngoc Anh gives 135 km on the Vam Co Tay. The differences come from which river was measured, where the distance starts (river mouth or coastline), measured versus forecast values, and what counts as damage (yield loss or total loss). The handbook names the source of every number and never mixes sources within one series.

</Depth>

## 10.3 What satellites see: the dry-season EVI anomaly

Satellites cannot measure the salinity of river water. They only see the **effect on plants**: rice stunted by salt, wilted, or fields left empty. A simple way to see this across the delta is the <Term id="evi-anomaly">EVI anomaly</Term>: the February–April mean EVI of one year minus the median February–April EVI of the 15 years 2010–2024. EVI is the vegetation index covered in [rs-knowledge, Chapter 6](https://quanta1985.github.io/rs-knowledge/en/ch06-spectral/) and Chapter 4.

In 2016, 22.7 % of the delta (about 890 thousand ha) had dry-season EVI at least 0.05 below normal. In 2020 it was 31.1 % (about 1.21 million ha), in 2024 18.6 %. That order matches the severity of the three events. A closer look at the table shows something odd, though.

Ben Tre was hit hardest by salt but had only 12.7 % anomalous area in 2016. Most of Ben Tre is coconut and fruit gardens whose canopy stays green all year, so EVI barely moves even when the trees suffer. An Giang, in contrast, gets no salt but shows 40–55 % anomalous area every year. February–April is when its winter–spring rice is harvested, and sowing a few weeks earlier or later than usual is enough to shift the February–April EVI a lot (see the start-of-season map in [Chapter 4](../rice/)).

<Note kind="key">

An EVI anomaly says that **plants are less green than usual**, not **why**. Early harvest to avoid salt, fallowing, drought, salinity and crop change all lower EVI. Statements about salinity need gauge measurements, crop calendars and local knowledge as well.

</Note>

<Depth level="advanced" title="Weak points of this calculation">

- **The 2010–2024 baseline contains the salinity years.** A median is robust to a few bad years, but if many recent years were all sown early, "normal" shifts too.
- **No rice mask.** The layer covers all land inside the provinces, including gardens, mangroves, shrimp ponds and towns. Chapter 4 has a rice map to filter with.
- **Clouds.** February–April is fairly clear in the Mekong Delta, but pixels with SummaryQA > 1 are dropped, so some places have few images.
- **250 m.** One MODIS pixel holds many fields, canals and houses; a single fallow field is hard to see.

</Depth>

## 10.4 Soil salinity indices: where do they work?

Many papers propose detecting saline soil with band indices. Two common ones are the <Term id="salinity-index">salinity index SI</Term> and <Term id="ndsi">NDSI</Term>:

<Formula label="Two common salinity indices" tex="\begin{gathered} SI = \sqrt{B \cdot R} \cr NDSI = \frac{R - NIR}{R + NIR} = -\,NDVI \end{gathered}" where="
B,\ R,\ NIR :: surface reflectance in blue, red and near infrared (Sentinel-2: B2, B4, B8)
" />

The idea behind SI is that salt crusts on bare soil make it brighter in the visible bands (Khan et al. 2001). NDSI, as the formula shows, is **exactly NDVI with the sign flipped**.

In the Ben Tre frame the median NDVI is 0.75: almost everything is green coconut and fruit gardens. Only 3.5 % of the frame has NDVI below 0.2. SI and NDVI correlate at **r = −0.80** over more than a million pixels. In other words SI mostly measures "where there are no plants", and those places are rivers, canals, roads and roofs, not saline soil.

  slider={{ min: -1, max: 1, step: 0.05 }} answer={-0.8} tol={0.1}

<Note kind="myth">

**Misconception:** "An NDSI or SI map from Sentinel-2 shows which fields are saline."

**In fact:** NDSI is exactly −NDVI, so it is just a greenness map with the colours reversed. SI only makes sense on dry bare soil with salt crusts, which is rare in Mekong rice fields and gardens. Salinity here travels mainly in the irrigation **water**, and water, plants and roofs all hide the soil signal. Knowing the salinity means measuring EC at a gauge or in the field.

</Note>

<Note kind="key">

Optical soil salinity indices only work on dry bare soil. In the Mekong Delta, where salt moves with the water and the ground is almost always covered by plants or water, these indices mostly repeat what NDVI already says.

</Note>

## 10.5 Coastal land-use change

After the salinity events, many coastal households made a <Term id="land-use-conversion">land-use conversion</Term>: from rice to coconut, fruit trees or shrimp, or to a <Term id="shrimp-rice">rice–shrimp</Term> rotation. Satellites follow this kind of change far better than they follow salinity. The <Term id="dynamic-world">Dynamic World</Term> dataset labels the land cover of every Sentinel-2 image at 10 m (Brown et al. 2022). Taking the most frequent label of the year gives one land-cover map per year.

## 10.6 Adaptation

After 2016 the response to salinity shifted from "fighting" to "living with" it. <Term id="resolution-120">Resolution 120/NQ-CP</Term> of 17 November 2017 on sustainable, climate-resilient development of the Mekong Delta chose to **work with nature**: fresh, brackish and saline water are all resources, and production follows ecological zones.

A <Term id="sluice-gate">salinity-control sluice</Term> closes when the tide brings salt in and opens to take in fresh water or to drain. It protects the area behind it but also holds water back, so it must follow an operating procedure issued by MARD (for Cai Lon – Cai Be, Decision 1834/QĐ-BNN-TL of 2024).

There is no single fix. Forecasts and crop calendars cut damage fastest and cheapest; large sluices protect wide areas but must be run well; changing what is farmed is the long-term answer. Remote sensing helps track calendars, water in canals and land use, but it does not replace salinity gauges.

</Note>
