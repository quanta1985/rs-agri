## 4.1 Rice in the two deltas

In both deltas most of the farmland grows <Term id="rice">paddy rice</Term>, but the two regions follow different rhythms.

- **The Red River Delta** has a cool winter. Most fields grow two <Term id="rice-season">crops</Term> a year: the **spring** crop, transplanted after Tết (Lunar New Year) and harvested around June, and the **main** (summer) crop, transplanted in June–July and harvested in October. Most farmers <Term id="transplanting">transplant</Term> seedlings.
- **The Mekong Delta** is warm all year, so water sets the number of crops. The **winter–spring** crop is sown as the flood recedes (November–December), the **summer–autumn** crop in April–May. Where high dykes keep the August flood out, farmers add an **autumn–winter** crop. Most of them use <Term id="direct-seeding">direct seeding</Term>.

The two maps come from different data. In the Mekong Delta the 250 m MODIS EVI series is dense enough: each EVI peak is one crop. In the Red River Delta only 7–12 % of MODIS observations were cloud-free in June–July 2024, so the spring and main crops merge in the EVI series. The northern map therefore counts crops with Sentinel-1 radar: every time a field is flooded for transplanting (VH drops to a trough) and then greens up (VH rises), that is one crop.

<Depth level="advanced" title="Comparing the map with statistics: different units">

Rice statistics count the **sown area** of each crop, so a hectare cropped three times counts three times in the annual total. The seasons map counts **land area**. To compare with an annual figure, multiply each class area by its number of crops. For 2024 that gives 3.71 million "crop-hectares" in the Mekong Delta and 0.80 million in the Red River Delta, taking the "3 or more" class as exactly 3.

A single season is easier to compare. The statistics for winter–spring 2024: Mekong Delta 1,487.7 thousand ha, Red River Delta 471.4 thousand ha (GSO 2024). The maps show 1,692 thousand ha (Mekong) and 469 thousand ha (Red River) of farmland with at least one crop. The close match in the north is partly luck: the map frame cuts off eastern Quang Ninh, and the "1 crop" class also holds fields that grow only the main crop.

</Depth>

## 4.2 The rice plant seen from space

A rice crop leaves a very clear trace in satellite images, because it starts as a sheet of water.

<Steps title="One rice crop through the satellite's eyes" items={[

"Flooded just before the canopy greens up" is the key to telling rice from other crops. Xiao et al. (2006) used exactly this sign with MODIS: a pixel whose <Term id="lswi">LSWI</Term> comes close to or exceeds its <Term id="evi">EVI</Term> early in the season is flooded. On radar the same sign is a deep <Term id="vh-backscatter">VH</Term> trough followed by a fast rise.

<Depth level="advanced" title="Why VH rises as rice grows while VV rises less">

Over a freshly planted paddy the radar wave is mostly reflected away like a mirror, so both VV and VH are dark. As rice tillers, the wave bounces many times inside the canopy (volume scattering) and between upright stems and the water. Volume scattering changes the polarisation, so VH rises strongly. VV also carries a double-bounce term between upright stems and water, and that term weakens as the canopy thickens, so VV rises less and less regularly. The FAO handbook (2017, section 5.2.3) describes the same sequence: backscatter climbs with tillering up to the reproductive stage and then levels off. Scattering and polarisation are covered in the <a href="https://quanta1985.github.io/rs-knowledge/en/ch11-sar/">SAR chapter of rs-knowledge</a>.

</Depth>

## 4.3 Crop calendars from time series

A <Term id="crop-calendar">crop calendar</Term> tells you when each area sows, heads and harvests. It feeds yield forecasts, irrigation scheduling and the timing of sowing to avoid salinity (Chapter 10). From an image series one estimates three <Term id="phenology">phenological</Term> dates: the <Term id="sos">start of season (SOS)</Term>, the <Term id="pos">peak of season (POS)</Term> and the <Term id="eos">end of season (EOS)</Term>.

An EVI series carries noise from leftover cloud, so gaps are filled and the series is smoothed first, here with a <Term id="savitzky-golay">Savitzky–Golay filter</Term>. Then comes the hard question: **when does a season "start"?** A common answer (Jönsson and Eklundh 2004) takes the pre-season trough, the peak and the amplitude A between them, and defines SOS as the first date the series climbs past a level:

<Formula label="Start of season from an amplitude threshold" tex="\mathrm{SOS}_f = \min\{\, t : \mathrm{EVI}(t) \ge \mathrm{EVI}_{\text{min}} + f\,A \,\}, \qquad A = \mathrm{EVI}_{\text{max}} - \mathrm{EVI}_{\text{min}}" where="
f :: fraction of the amplitude: 0 (as soon as the series leaves the trough), 0.2 or 0.5
A :: EVI amplitude of the season
\mathrm{EVI}_{\text{min}},\ \mathrm{EVI}_{\text{max}} :: pre-season trough and seasonal peak of the smoothed series
" />

Which threshold is right? None is right everywhere. f = 0 tracks the first greening but reacts to noise at the trough. f = 0.5 is steadier but late, because by then the rice is tillering. To match sowing dates recorded in the field, the threshold has to be calibrated on fields with farm records. In the Red River Delta the VH trough marks transplanting more directly, but only to within Sentinel-1's 12-day step. Sakamoto et al. (2006) mapped rice phenology across the Mekong Delta from MODIS in a similar way and showed that the crop calendar there follows the flood rhythm of the Tien and Hau rivers.

## 4.4 Mapping rice with Sentinel-1

The FAO handbook (2017, section 2.5.3) judges that dense, regular SAR series such as Sentinel-1 have made irrigated rice mapping a mature technique. IRRI's PRISM project in the Philippines, Asia-RiCE (JAXA) and GEO-Rice (ESA) all rely on radar series. Section 5.2.3 of the book outlines the workflow: calibrate, filter speckle, co-register at least three images about 20–25 days apart (before, during and after planting), and write decision rules on the backscatter profile.

This section uses a simpler rule with a single number: the **seasonal VH range**, the 90th minus the 10th percentile of the in-season VH series (after a 3 × 3 smoothing in linear power). Rice goes from a flooded trough (−25 dB) to a dense canopy (−15 dB), so its range is large. Buildings, tree crops and permanent water change little.

The table carries the main lesson of this section: **the area depends on the threshold**. Lowering it by 4 dB adds 2,600 ha of rice in An Giang. Otsu only finds the natural split of the histogram; it does not guarantee the true boundary between rice and non-rice. In the small fields of the Red River Delta many 10 m pixels fall on bunds, village roads and ditches, which dilutes the range.

<Note kind="key">

Rice has a very distinctive radar series, a flooded trough followed by a strong rise, so a single statistic such as the seasonal VH range already separates most paddies. But the area moves by thousands of hectares when the threshold changes, so the map has to be checked before it is used for area (section 4.5).

</Note>

<Depth level="advanced" title="The FAO three-date rule and why it is not used here">

The FAO handbook (2017, Box 2, section 5.2.3) quotes decision rules for Mirzapur district (India) based on σ⁰ at three dates L1, L2, L3. Water, for example, is any pixel below −17 dB on all three dates; early transplanted rice rises by more than 1 dB at each step within given ranges. Those ranges belong to one sensor, one incidence angle and one crop calendar, so they cannot be copied to the Mekong Delta. With a 12-day Sentinel-1 series, statistics that summarise the whole series (range, trough, rate of rise) are more robust than three isolated dates. Nguyen et al. (2016) used Sentinel-1A series to map rice extent and cropping intensity across the Mekong Delta.

</Depth>

## 4.5 From map to area

The easiest way to get an area is **pixel counting**: add up the area of every pixel classified as rice. The FAO handbook (2017, section 5.2.1.1) calls this direct estimation. The handbook's annex (by Gallego) explains the catch: the <Term id="pixel-counting-bias">bias of pixel counting</Term> equals **commission** (non-rice labelled rice) minus **omission** (rice labelled non-rice), and no theorem makes the two cancel. Mixed pixels at field edges make it worse. Waldner and Defourny (2017, cited in the annex) found that with Landsat-8, mixed pixels alone could bias the crop area by more than 10 % across much of South Africa, even with every pure pixel classified correctly.

The fix is to check a **sample of points**. Cross-tabulating map class against true class at those points gives a <Term id="confusion-matrix">confusion matrix</Term>. Its rows give the <Term id="user-accuracy">user's accuracy</Term>, its columns the <Term id="producer-accuracy">producer's accuracy</Term>. If the points come from a <Term id="stratified-sample">stratified sample</Term> with the map classes as strata, the matrix yields the true area and its standard error (Olofsson et al. 2014). This is the "direct calibration" estimator of the FAO annex.

<Formula label="Bias-corrected area from a stratified sample" tex="\hat p_{ij} = W_i\,\frac{n_{ij}}{n_{i\cdot}}, \qquad \hat A_j = A_{\text{tot}} \sum_i \hat p_{ij}, \qquad \widehat{SE}(\hat A_j) = A_{\text{tot}} \sqrt{\sum_i W_i^2\,\frac{\frac{n_{ij}}{n_{i\cdot}}\left(1-\frac{n_{ij}}{n_{i\cdot}}\right)}{n_{i\cdot}-1}}" where="
A_{\text{tot}} :: total map area
W_i :: share of the map area in class i (known from the map)
n_{ij} :: number of points mapped as class i whose reference class is j
n_{i\cdot} :: number of points drawn in map class i
\hat A_j :: estimated area of class j; 95 % confidence interval ≈ ± 1.96 SE
" />

<Note kind="key">

Pixel counting gives a biased area, and the bias is commission minus omission; it does not cancel by itself. A trustworthy area needs a check sample drawn at random within strata, and a corrected estimate with a confidence interval. The map tells you where the rice is; the sample tells you how much.

</Note>

<Depth level="advanced" title="Direct calibration, inverse calibration and the regression estimator">

The FAO annex compares three ways of correcting the bias. **Direct calibration** (the formula above) uses the probability "true class j given map class i" and fits a sample stratified by map class. **Inverse calibration** uses "map class i given true class j" and inverts the matrix; it becomes unstable when the matrix is close to singular. The **regression estimator** uses large sample segments (for example 300 × 300 m or 500 × 500 m) with the crop share measured in the field and the share on the map. The annex considers it probably the safest option, because it tolerates location errors and naturally includes mixed pixels. Chapter 5 (Sampling frames & area estimation) treats it in depth.

The annex also warns of a trap: if analysts know the expected figure (say, the official statistic) and tune the classifier until the pixel count matches, the counted area is no longer objective. The map still answers "where is the rice?", but it is no longer an independent area estimate.

</Depth>
