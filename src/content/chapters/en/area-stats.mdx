Chapter 4 counted 1,692 thousand ha with rice on the 2024 Mekong map, 14 % more than the winter–spring sown area published by the General Statistics Office. Which number is right? A map, however good it looks, does not say how wrong it is. Official statistics need a number with a **standard error**, and that takes a **probability sample** in the field. This chapter follows chapters 3 and 5 of the FAO (2017) handbook: satellite images are used to build and stratify the sampling frame, and then as an auxiliary variable that shrinks the error of the estimate.

## 5.1 From a map to a statistic

There are two ways to turn a map into an area. The first is **pixel counting**: rice pixels times the pixel area. It is quick, but biased by classification errors and mixed pixels, and it says nothing about its own error (Chapter 4, section 4.5). The second takes a **field sample** as the measurement and uses the map to make the sample more efficient. The FAO handbook (2017, section 5.3) describes it as combining "exhaustive but inaccurate information from satellite images with accurate information on a sample".

<Note kind="key">

The probability field sample is the "truth"; the map is an **auxiliary variable**. A good map gives a smaller error for the same number of field visits. A poor map only loses that advantage; it does not bias the estimate, as long as the map is used **before** the sample is drawn and by a fixed rule.

</Note>

## 5.2 List frames and area frames

A <Term id="list-frame">list frame</Term> is a list of households or farms from a census or register. It is convenient for asking about output, prices and labour, but it is only as good as the list: new farms are missing, some are duplicated, and it ages fast. An <Term id="area-frame">area frame</Term> uses the land itself as the unit: points, transects or <Term id="segment">segments</Term>. It covers the territory with no gaps or overlaps and does not need to know who farms. The line between the two is not sharp: many countries use enumeration areas as the first stage of a list frame, and enumeration areas with boundaries in a GIS behave like an area frame (FAO 2017, section 3.1).

| Area frame type | Unit | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Segments with physical boundaries | block bounded by roads, canals | surveyors find the boundary easily | much photo-interpretation to draw them |
| Square grid | square cell on a projection | cheap, built in a GIS | cell edges cut across fields |
| Points | one point, cover recorded there | very cheap, suits small fields | little information per point |
| Clustered points | a few nearby points | less travel | points in a cluster are alike |

A rule of thumb quoted in the FAO handbook: a segment should contain on average 10–20 fields or cover patches, so a surveyor can finish it in under half a day (Taylor et al., 1997). Fields in both deltas are small (Chapter 2), so the 250 m cells used below are a reasonable size: one 6.25 ha cell holds dozens of fields.

## 5.3 Sampling a real rice map

The simulation below treats the 2024 rice map of Chapter 4 as the "truth": each 250 m cell inside the region outline has y = its rice area (the whole cell if the map gives at least one season, 0 if not). The Mekong Delta has 636,121 cells, 42.7 % of them with rice, 1,692 thousand ha in all. We pretend not to know that total, draw a sample of cells, "visit" them to read y, and estimate the total.

The auxiliary variable x is the cropland area of the cell on the Esri 2021 map (Chapter 3). WorldCover is not used, because WorldCover itself delimited the cropland when the rice map was made: as the auxiliary variable it would leave the 'little cropland' stratum with no rice by construction, and stratification would look more efficient than it is. Strata follow the Esri cropland share: below 25 %, 25–75 %, 75 % and above. In the Mekong Delta the three strata have 228,418 / 112,594 / 295,109 cells, and 2.2 / 30.9 / 78.4 % of their cells have rice.

<Formula label="Simple random sample" tex="\hat{Y} = N\,\bar{y}, \qquad SE(\hat{Y}) = N\sqrt{\left(1-\tfrac{n}{N}\right)\tfrac{s_y^2}{n}}" where="N: cells in the region, n: sampled cells, ȳ and s_y²: mean and variance of y in the sample." />

<Formula label="Stratified sample" tex="\hat{Y}_{st} = \sum_h N_h\,\bar{y}_h, \qquad SE^2 = \sum_h N_h^2\left(1-\tfrac{n_h}{N_h}\right)\tfrac{s_h^2}{n_h}" where="h: stratum. Proportional allocation: n_h ∝ N_h. Neyman allocation: n_h ∝ N_h·S_h, so variable strata get more cells." />

With 200 cells in the Mekong Delta, the simple random sample gives a standard deviation of 141.2 thousand ha over 500 repeats, a coefficient of variation of 8.3 %. Proportional stratification brings it down to 97.7, Neyman stratification to 88.8 and the regression estimator to 97.0 thousand ha. The mean of the 500 estimates sits close to the true value for all four: they are unbiased and differ only in spread. The standard error computed from each single sample also matches the real spread (on average 138.6 against 141.2 thousand ha for the simple sample), so one sample already tells how precise it is. The Neyman allocation here uses the true standard deviation of each stratum, which a real survey does not know in advance; in the field S_h comes from a pilot or an earlier survey, so 2.53× is the best case.

<Note kind="tip">

Rice in the Red River Delta is scarcer and more scattered: 27.9 % of the cells have rice, against 42.7 % in the Mekong. With the same 200 cells, the coefficient of variation is 11.1 % in the Red River Delta and 8.3 % in the Mekong. The smaller and more scattered the target, the more cells it needs, and the more the satellite is worth: Neyman stratification there reaches an efficiency of 2.77×.

</Note>

## 5.4 Regression, calibration and small areas

The <Term id="regression-estimator">regression estimator</Term> fits a line y = a + b·x on the sampled cells, then plugs in the true mean of x over the whole region, known because the map covers every cell:

<Formula label="Regression estimator (FAO 2017, section 5.3)" tex="\hat{\bar{Y}}_{reg} = \bar{y} + b\,(\bar{X} - \bar{x}), \qquad E \approx \frac{1}{1-r^2}" where="ȳ, x̄: sample means; X̄: mean of x over all N cells; b: slope of y on x in the sample; r: correlation between y and x; E: efficiency relative to a simple random sample." />

In the Mekong Delta the per-cell correlation between rice area and Esri cropland area is r = 0.72, so theory promises an efficiency of about 2.1×; the simulation gives 2.12×. The FAO handbook cites two operational examples: rice area estimation in Afghanistan, with R² = 0.96 between rice pixels and rice area in the sample segments, and China's CAERSS project, with coefficients of variation of 4.2–7.0 % for maize, rice and soybean.

<Term id="relative-efficiency">Relative efficiency</Term> translates directly into money: an efficiency of 2× means the same precision with half the field segments.

Two other families also use the map as an auxiliary variable:

- **Calibration estimators** use a confusion matrix from a check sample to correct pixel-counted area, through user's accuracy (direct estimator) or producer's accuracy (inverse estimator). Figure 4.5 of Chapter 4 does exactly this, following Olofsson et al. (2014). The condition: the matrix must come from a probability sample, and the extrapolation must follow the sampling design.
- **<Term id="small-area-estimation">Small area estimation</Term>** is used when numbers are needed for districts or communes with only a few sampled cells each. A regression model with satellite covariates "borrows" information from other districts (FAO 2017, section 5.5).

<Depth level="advanced">

**Why regression beats stratification here.** Stratification uses x only to form groups (three levels), while regression uses each cell's own x. When the relation between x and y is close to linear, regression extracts more information. The two can be combined: a separate regression in each stratum (the stratified regression estimator), as in the formula of Sud et al. (2015) presented in the FAO handbook. The regression estimator is slightly biased in small samples (of order 1/n), negligible with a few hundred cells.

</Depth>

## 5.5 Sample size and cost

For a rice area with a given <Term id="coefficient-of-variation">coefficient of variation</Term> CV, a simple random sample needs:

<Formula label="Sample size for a target CV" tex="n_0 = \left(\frac{S_y}{CV\cdot\bar{Y}}\right)^2, \qquad n = \frac{n_0}{1 + n_0/N}" where="S_y: standard deviation of y between cells, Ȳ: mean of y. Stratification or regression divide n by their efficiency." />

In the Mekong Delta a 5 % CV needs 538 and a 2 % CV 3,343 random 250 m cells. The Red River Delta has fewer and more scattered rice cells, so it needs 1,030 and 6,306. Divided by the regression estimator's efficiency, these numbers shrink accordingly. They are for a whole delta; the same CV in every province needs roughly that sample in each province, and the cost multiplies by the number of provinces. The FAO handbook (section 5.7) stresses that satellites pay off only when the efficiency gain exceeds the cost of images and processing; with free Sentinel and Landsat images the balance has tipped firmly towards using them.

<Note kind="warn">

This simulation is lenient in one respect: the "field measurement" y comes from a map, so there is no measurement error, no cell is missed and every cell can be reached. In the field there are surveyor errors, unreachable cells and segment boundaries that are hard to find on the ground. These errors do not go away when satellites are added, and they can cause real bias.

</Note>

In Viet Nam, sown rice area still comes from the statistical system's reports and surveys. Rice remote sensing is present through GEOGLAM's Asia-RiCE component, led by JAXA: a radar rice monitoring scheme over the Mekong Delta with ALOS-2 and Sentinel-1, with the Vietnam National Space Center (VNSC), the Vietnam Academy of Science and Technology and the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Development (CEOS, 2016). The gap between the map and the official figure (Chapter 4: +14 %) is exactly what a satellite-assisted area frame could narrow.

<Note kind="key">

Satellites do not replace surveys; they make surveys cheaper and give numbers with errors attached. Three steps: build and stratify the frame with a map, measure y on a probability sample, use the map as an auxiliary variable. The standard error falls only with the square root of the sample, so a good auxiliary variable is worth hundreds of field trips.

</Note>
