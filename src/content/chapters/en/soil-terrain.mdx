The floods, droughts and salt of the previous chapters all play out on a particular ground: young alluvium, acid in many places, and so low that a few tens of centimetres matter. This chapter measures that ground from space and shows where the measurement can be trusted and where it cannot.

## 11.1 The soils of the two deltas

Both deltas are **young alluvium** laid down by the Red River and the Mekong over the last few thousand years. Fertile alluvial soils line the big rivers. In the low basins away from the rivers, once brackish swamps, lie **acid sulfate soils**. Saline soils fringe the coast, and peat forms in a few places that stay flooded for long periods.

The FAO handbook (2017, section 1.4, Table 2) lists a **soil map** among the reference layers needed before doing agricultural statistics with satellite imagery, at a minimum scale of 1:100,000, for land suitability and erosion and salinity risks. Where no open national map exists, the easiest source is <Term id="soilgrids">SoilGrids</Term> 2.0 (Poggio et al., 2021): a global map at 250 m, six depths down to 2 m, licensed CC BY 4.0.

But 250 m matches a map at about 1:500,000 (Tobler's rule: cell size in metres equals the scale denominator divided by 2,000), five times coarser than FAO asks for. And SoilGrids is a **prediction** by a machine-learning model trained on the soil profiles in international databases and on environmental covariates, not a measurement.

<Term id="soil-organic-carbon">Organic carbon</Term> peaks on the coastal mud flats of both deltas (93.5 g/kg in the Mekong, 95.5 g/kg in the Red River Delta, against medians of 30.5 and 20 g/kg), where mangrove leaves and roots build up in tidal mud. Clay in the Mekong Delta is almost uniform (median 38.5 %): this map cannot tell a river levee from a back swamp, something any farmer knows.

<Note kind="warn">

SoilGrids is useful for a first look where there is no soil map. It does not replace a national soil survey, and it cannot map acid sulfate soils: extreme values are smoothed away, because the model learns from few profiles in the delta. To know which field is acid, auger and measure.

</Note>

## 11.2 Acid sulfate soils: satellites see the effects, not the pyrite

<Term id="acid-sulfate-soil">Acid sulfate soils</Term> contain pyrite (FeS₂) formed under former mangroves and brackish swamps. While waterlogged, the pyrite is stable. When the soil dries (dry season, canal digging, raised beds), air gets in and the pyrite oxidises to sulfuric acid:

<Formula label="Pyrite oxidation" tex="\mathrm{FeS_2} + \tfrac{15}{4}\,\mathrm{O_2} + \tfrac{7}{2}\,\mathrm{H_2O} \rightarrow \mathrm{Fe(OH)_3} + 2\,\mathrm{H_2SO_4}" where="
\mathrm{FeS_2} :: pyrite in the sediment below the surface
\mathrm{H_2SO_4} :: sulfuric acid that drops the pH below 4 and dissolves aluminium and iron, toxic to crops
" />

All of this happens underground. Optical images see only the top few millimetres, and C-band radar reaches only a few centimetres into moist soil. So satellites cannot measure pH. They see **signs**:

<Note kind="key">

Remote sensing helps **outline suspect areas** and **track the effects** of acid sulfate soils; it does not replace augering and measuring pH. A trustworthy acid sulfate map always has field points behind it.

</Note>

## 11.3 Elevation: surface models and terrain models

Every free global elevation model is measured from space, and most measure the **first surface** seen. SRTM (2000) and Copernicus DEM (from the TanDEM-X satellites) use radar, and radar bounces off tree crowns and roofs. That is a <Term id="dsm-dtm">surface model (DSM)</Term>. Flooding questions need a **terrain model (DTM)**: the bare ground.

In mountains, a few metres of error hardly matter. In a delta that is only one or two metres high, a few metres are the whole story. DeltaDTM (Pronk et al., 2024) corrects Copernicus DEM with lidar heights from the ICESat-2 and GEDI missions: it removes trees and buildings, then interpolates. Its mean absolute error on low coastal land below 10 m is 0.45 m.

The bands of large differences are the **sand ridges** (giồng) and river islands, where people plant orchards and coconut and build villages. Over open rice fields the two models agree closely.

<Note kind="warn">

**Elevation above what?** DeltaDTM and Copernicus DEM are measured from the <Term id="vertical-datum">EGM2008 geoid</Term>, while Vietnamese topographic maps use mean sea level at the Hon Dau tide gauge. Minderhoud et al. (2019) built a DEM from national survey data and found a mean elevation of 0.82 m above the Hon Dau datum for the Mekong Delta, while the median of DeltaDTM here is 1.9 m. That 1 m gap comes partly from the different datums and partly from DeltaDTM's remaining error; this chapter cannot separate the two. The lesson: before comparing a DEM with a water level, know which datum it uses.

</Note>

## 11.4 How much land lies below each water level

Adding up the area of the cells below each elevation gives a <Term id="hypsometric-curve">hypsometric curve</Term>. Reading it at a sea level gives the area lower than that level.

In DeltaDTM, 60 % of Mekong Delta land is below 2 m and 92.5 % below 3 m; in Copernicus DEM, only 38 % and 65 %. The Red River Delta is much higher: 10.9 % below 2 m (median elevation about 5.3 m, as the region includes the upper delta around Hanoi).

Below 1 m, DeltaDTM puts only 4 % of the Mekong Delta. Minderhoud et al. (2019), with their survey-based DEM, found that a 1 m sea-level rise would put 75 % of the Mekong Delta plain below sea level, against 31 % with the SRTM DEM. <mark>An offset of about 1 m in the elevation model turns "a small part" into "most of the delta".</mark> That is why every sea-level-rise map must state the elevation model and the datum it uses.

<Note kind="myth">

**"Below sea level means flooded."** Not quite. Sea dikes, river dikes and sluices keep the water out; large reclaimed areas of the Netherlands lie metres below sea level. But the lower the land sits against the water, the harder drainage gets, the more pumping costs, the easier salt seeps in, and the worse a dike breach becomes. The curve in Figure 11.3 measures **exposure**, not area certain to flood.

</Note>

## 11.5 Subsidence: the delta sinks faster than the sea rises

In the Mekong Delta, <Term id="land-subsidence">subsidence</Term> comes mainly from groundwater pumping: as water pressure in the aquifers falls, the clay layers between them compact and the ground drops, for good. Erban et al. (2014) used 78 ALOS PALSAR radar interferograms from 2006–2010 (<Term id="insar">InSAR</Term>) and measured subsidence of about 1–4 cm/yr over areas of thousands of km². Minderhoud et al. (2017) modelled 25 years of pumping (1991–2016): the delta sank about 18 cm on average, at a current mean rate of 1.1 cm/yr and over 2.5 cm/yr in places.

For comparison: global mean sea level rose by 3.7 mm/yr over 2006–2018 (IPCC, 2021). What decides flooding is <Term id="relative-slr">relative sea-level rise</Term>, the rising sea plus the sinking land:

<Formula label="Relative sea-level rise" tex="\Delta \mathrm{RSL}(t) = \Delta \mathrm{SL}(t) + S \cdot t" where="
\Delta \mathrm{SL}(t) :: absolute sea-level rise after t years (from a scenario)
S :: local subsidence rate (cm/yr)
t :: years since the base year
" />

Minderhoud et al. (2020) ran two groups of groundwater-pumping scenarios to 2100: group B (pumping following current trends) and group M (reduced pumping). Additional subsidence from 2018 to 2100 is 68 cm (B1) and about 1 m (B2, 86–110 cm) in group B, but only 12–39 cm in group M. With the median absolute sea-level rise of RCP 4.5, the share of the Mekong Delta still above sea level at the end of the century is 32 % in B1, 44 % in M1 and 57 % in M3. How groundwater is pumped over the next few decades decides nearly a quarter of the delta.

<Depth level="advanced" title="Why this chapter does not measure subsidence itself">

InSAR needs the **phase** of the radar signal. Sentinel-1 on Google Earth Engine is the GRD product: intensity only, the phase is gone. Measuring subsidence means downloading SLC images (tens of GB per scene) and processing them with software such as SNAP, ISCE or MintPy, or using processed products from projects such as LiCSAR. Rice fields add a difficulty: the field surface changes with the crop cycle, so the phase decorrelates quickly; analysts rely on stable reflectors such as houses, roads and dikes (persistent scatterer InSAR, PS-InSAR).

</Depth>
