In 2001, winter–spring rice in the Mekong Delta yielded 5.04 t/ha on average; in 2024 it yielded 7.23 t/ha on 1,487.7 thousand ha sown. Most of that rise came from varieties, fertiliser, irrigation and machines, steadily year after year. Early warning does not ask whether yields are rising, but whether **this year** will be above or below the usual level, and asks early enough to act. This chapter follows chapter 6 of the FAO (2017) handbook, on early warning systems and yield forecasting, and tries it on real data from both deltas.

## 6.1 What early warning is

In the FAO handbook every <Term id="early-warning">early warning system</Term> has three parts: **data collection**, **analysis and forecasting**, and **dissemination** to the people who must act. There is a built-in tension between them: the longer one waits for data, the surer the forecast, but the less time is left to act. Remote sensing helps with the first part: it watches every field, regularly, without waiting for reports from below.

<Term id="crop-yield">Yield</Term> in Vietnamese statistics is production divided by **sown area**. A crop that fails completely stays in the area, so that province's yield drops close to zero. That is exactly what early warning wants to see coming.

<Note kind="key">

A useful yield forecast must say: how far this year departs from the trend; how wrong the forecast is on average over years the model has not seen; and how long before harvest it is available. Without all three it is not yet early warning.

</Note>

In the inland provinces the trend explains most of the variation: Kien Giang gains 0.102 t/ha a year (R² = 0.72), Long An 0.089 t/ha (R² = 0.80). The FAO handbook makes the same point: where agriculture is intensive, the trend carries most of the yield variation, and what is left after removing it is mostly weather. The coast is different. According to the National Statistics Office, Ben Tre sowed 15.1 thousand ha of winter–spring rice in 2016 and harvested 0.8 thousand tonnes, 0.05 t/ha; in 2020, 0.04 t/ha on 5.3 thousand ha. Tra Vinh fell to 4.04 and 3.54 t/ha, against about 6.6–6.8 t/ha in normal years. These are the two drought and salinity seasons of Chapter 10.

In the Mekong Delta winter–spring rice is sown after the flood, so mean cropland EVI peaks around day 63 after 15 November, that is mid-January (between day 55 and 103 depending on the year), then drops fast as the crop is harvested in February–March. The dry season is clear: only 14 % of the composites are dropped for having under half of the cells with good data. The Red River Delta is the opposite. Spring rice is transplanted in February, the season of drizzle: 39 % of the composites are dropped, and in February–March on average only 43 % of the cells have good data. EVI peaks around day 75 after 15 February, in late April. The Red River curve is broken, and its peak EVI depends heavily on which images got through the cloud.

<Note kind="tip">

To know whether a season is going well, one must know **which stage** it is at. The same EVI of 0.4 can be rice greening up or rice half harvested. That is why yield models always go with the crop calendar (Chapter 4).

</Note>

## 6.4 A simple yield model

The simplest remote-sensing model in the FAO handbook (section 6.4.2) is a <Term id="yield-forecast">yield function</Term>: regress past official yields on a vegetation index, then plug in this year's index to forecast. Here the index is the <Term id="peak-evi">seasonal peak EVI</Term>, averaged over the province's cropland. Both series are first <Term id="yield-trend">detrended</Term>, and the model is scored with <Term id="loo-cv">leave-one-year-out cross-validation</Term>: each year is forecast with the line fitted to the other years.

<Formula label="Yield function on departures from the trend" tex="\Delta Y_t = a + b\,\Delta EVI_t, \qquad RMSE_{LOO} = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_t \big(\Delta Y_t - \hat{\Delta Y}_{t,(-t)}\big)^2}" where="ΔY_t, ΔEVI_t: yield and index in year t minus the trend line; Ŷ_(−t): the forecast for year t from the model fitted to the other years. Compare RMSE_LOO with the error of just guessing the mean of the other years." />

The results are modest. With peak EVI, the detrended R² is highest in An Giang (0.29) and Kien Giang (0.22); in An Giang the leave-one-out error is 0.31 t/ha, against 0.35 t/ha for guessing the mean. The model beats the mean in 6 of 13 provinces. Pooling all provinces, R² is only 0.01. Son et al. (2014) used MODIS EVI for the Mekong Delta in 2002–2011 and reported R² of 0.62–0.71 for winter–spring rice; values of that size appear here too **before** the trend is removed (Long An 0.74, Kien Giang 0.75, Soc Trang 0.73), so comparing the two results requires knowing how the trend was handled.

In the Red River Delta the model beats the mean in 8 of 11 provinces, best in Hung Yen (R² = 0.48; error 0.12 against 0.16 t/ha). That sounds better than the Mekong, but spring yields there vary very little around the trend: guessing the mean is already off by only 0.08 t/ha in Nam Dinh and 0.40 t/ha in Vinh Phuc. In Hung Yen the satellite removes only 0.04 t/ha of error.

<Note kind="warn">

Why the simple model is weak here: a 250 m MODIS cell mixes rice with orchards, villages and canals; a province average dilutes the damaged areas; early-season cloud leaves gaps; and the yield statistics have errors of their own. Operational models add weather, 10 m images, seasonal rice maps, and calibration with crop-cut surveys.

</Note>

## 6.5 How early: the timing of the hazard

In Ben Tre and Tra Vinh, peak EVI is almost unrelated to the failed years: R² of 0.01 and 0.00. Winter–spring rice greens up normally in December–January, then salt water moves up the rivers in February–March, just as the crop heads and fills grain. The EVI peak is over before the damage is done. With **mean EVI 1 Feb – 31 Mar** the detrended R² is 0.24 in both provinces, and the leave-one-out error is slightly below that of guessing the mean (Ben Tre 1.40 against 1.51 t/ha; Tra Vinh 0.80 against 0.85). Take this as a hint only: 24 years of data, a result driven mostly by the two failed seasons, and an index chosen after trying two on 13 provinces. Ben Tre's winter–spring rice area has also become very small (12.8–17.2 thousand ha in 2015, 2016 and 2019, but 0.77 thousand ha in 2024), so in recent years the province's cropland EVI mostly does not measure rice. Ben Tre's February–March EVI was 0.30 in 2016 and 0.25 in 2020, against 0.37 in 2015 and 0.39 in 2019. In An Giang, though, the late-season index does not help (R² 0.02). And a low February–March EVI has more than one meaning: rice killed by salt, but also fields harvested early to escape the salt (Chapter 10), or never sown. The index says the crop is less green, not why.

<Depth level="advanced">

**Agrometeorological models.** The FAO handbook (section 6.4.1) describes another route: compute crop variables from the weather, such as actual evapotranspiration or the water requirement satisfaction index, then regress yield on them (an <Term id="agromet-model">agrometeorological model</Term>). In Morocco, seasonal rainfall alone explains most of the variation in wheat yield; in Bangladesh, a rice model uses actual evapotranspiration and the August maximum temperature. In our two deltas rice is irrigated, so rainfall explains little; the main hazards are salinity, floods, storms and pests, which a simple water model does not see. The data for this route are in Chapters 7 and 9.

</Depth>

## 6.6 From warning to action

A warning is only worth something if someone acts on it. The FAO handbook devotes section 6.3 to "early warning, early action": separating the stage of livelihood stress from the emergency, and acting already in the first stage. In the Mekong Delta the lesson of 2016 turned into action in the dry seasons that followed: salinity forecasts are issued from the start of the season, and winter–spring sowing is moved earlier so the crop heads before the salt arrives (Chapter 10).

<Note kind="key">

Satellites help early warning most when used to know **where** and **how many hectares** are at risk, on the crop calendar. Yield forecasts from a single index are weak: the index has to be measured when the hazard strikes, and the model has to be scored on years it has not seen.

</Note>
