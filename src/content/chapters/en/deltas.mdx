Viet Nam has two large deltas, one at each end of the country. **The Red River Delta** in the north has a cool winter, fields behind dykes and a very dense population. **The Mekong Delta** in the south is warm all year, lives with the Mekong flood pulse and is invaded by salt water every dry season. This handbook uses both regions as the example for every method: each figure shows real satellite data from the two deltas, not an illustration.

Three things stand out on the map:

- **The Red River Delta has more tree cover than expected (37 %).** It is not farmland but the forested hills of Quang Ninh, Ba Vi and Tam Dao and the limestone karst of Ninh Binh: the statistical region "Red River Delta" includes provinces with hills. The delta proper is the low land in the middle.
- **26 % of the Mekong Delta is tree cover.** Most of it is fruit and coconut orchards in Ben Tre, Tien Giang and Vinh Long, plus the U Minh melaleuca forest and the Ca Mau mangroves. Orchards are farmland, but WorldCover files them as trees because the canopy covers the ground. A land-cover map describes **what covers the ground**, not **how people use the land**.
- **The two regions differ sharply in people.** The mean density of the Red River Delta is more than twice the Mekong's. Each hectare of paddy there feeds more people.

<Note kind="warn">

At ~250 m each cell carries only the most common class of its 625 pixels of 10 m. Small and thin classes (canal-side villages, roads, ditches) are swallowed by their surroundings on the map. The shares in this chapter are therefore computed on a 30 m grid (sampled from the 10 m map), not by counting 250 m cells.

</Note>

## 1.2 Satellite numbers and statistical numbers

A gridded map lets you add up population or area inside any boundary. But it is a **model product**, not a count. <Term id="gridded-population">GHS-POP</Term> spreads the population of each administrative unit over 100 m cells according to the building area and volume seen from space.

This lesson comes back throughout the handbook. The rice area counted from the satellite map in Chapter 4 is about 14 % above the General Statistics Office figure. The water map after typhoon Yagi in Chapter 8 is ten times smaller than the reported damage. A satellite number means something only when you say **what it measures**, **at which resolution**, and **compared with what**.

<Note kind="key">

Satellite maps do not replace official statistics. They tell **where**, **when** and **how things change**, everywhere, regularly and cheaply. A correct area or production figure needs the map combined with a field reference sample (Chapter 4 section 4.5, Chapter 5).

</Note>

## 1.3 What remote sensing does for agricultural statistics

The FAO (2017) handbook this one builds on was written for statistical agencies. It points to four places where remote sensing helps most:

<Tiles items={[
  { t: 'Sampling frames', d: 'Land-cover maps help split the territory into strata and pick survey points or segments; less fieldwork where there are no fields.' },
  { t: 'Crop maps', d: 'Classifying seasonal image series shows which field grows what (Chapters 3, 4).' },
  { t: 'Area estimation', d: 'Map plus reference sample gives an area with a confidence interval and corrects pixel-counting bias (Chapter 5).' },
  { t: 'Early warning, yield', d: 'Vegetation-index and weather series follow the season and warn early of drought, flood, salinity (Chapters 6–10).' },
]} caption="After FAO (2017), Overview and Chapters 8–9." />

The book also stresses that the largest cost is **not the imagery**. Landsat, Sentinel and MODIS are free, and cloud processing needs almost no hardware. What costs is **people**: a team that knows GIS, image analysis, statistics, agronomy and meteorology (FAO, 2017, section 9.1). Chapter 2 deals with data and costs in detail.

<Depth level="advanced" title="Why agricultural statistics needs both surveys and satellites">

A field survey is right at the surveyed points but slow and expensive. A satellite map covers everything but has classification errors. The FAO standard is to treat a **probability sample** in the field as the truth and to use the map as an **auxiliary variable** that reduces the error: stratify by the map, then use a regression estimator or a confusion-matrix correction. With a good map, the same precision needs far fewer survey points. With a poor map the estimate is still unbiased; it just gains nothing. Chapter 5 simulates this on the real rice map.

</Depth>

## 1.5 How to use this handbook

- **Depth.** The *View* tab of the toolbar has three levels: *Basic* (default, hides the technical boxes), *Advanced* (shows every box) and *Reference* (keeps only headings and look-up tables, for quick revision). On a first reading, stay on Basic.
- **Prediction questions.** Most figures come after a question. Guess first, then read the answer: it is the best way to remember.
- **Interactive figures.** Each figure has a *Try* line suggesting what to do and a *Source* line with the data, image dates and processing. Every number in the text can be recomputed with the scripts in `scripts/`.
- **Labs.** Chapter 15 has Python notebooks (run on Google Colab) and Earth Engine scripts that run on real data.
- **Background.** Spectral principles, vegetation indices, radar and image classification are not rewritten here. To revise them, see the <a href="https://quanta1985.github.io/rs-knowledge/en/">Remote Sensing & GIS</a> handbook.
