Each chapter of Part C looked at one hazard: rain and heat (Chapter 7), floods (Chapter 8), drought (Chapter 9), salinity (Chapter 10), low land and subsidence (Chapter 11), the coast (Chapter 12). A planner has to look at all of them at once: which province faces the most hazards, and which hazard dominates where? The usual answer is to stack the layers with a set of weights. That is easy, but the final number depends on choices that are not in the data. This chapter builds such a map and then takes it apart to show those choices.

## 14.1 Hazard, exposure, vulnerability

The FAO handbook (2017, section 6.2) puts it plainly: a drought alone does not trigger an emergency; whether it becomes a disaster depends on its impact on communities and the environment, that is, on how vulnerable people are to the shock. Section 6.3 adds that disaster risk is the interaction between hazards and vulnerability, and both keep changing. The common framework today (IPCC, 2014; UNDRR, 2016) has three parts:

<Steps items={[
  { t: 'Hazard', d: 'The phenomenon that can do harm: floods, drought, salinity, storm surge. Remote sensing measures this part fairly well (surface water, rain, vegetation indices, elevation).' },
  { t: 'Exposure', d: 'What lies in the hazard zone: paddies, orchards, houses, people. Land cover (Chapter 3), rice maps (Chapter 4) and population (Chapter 1) answer this.' },
  { t: 'Vulnerability', d: 'How badly things suffer when the hazard comes: is there a high dyke, a salinity sluice, money to switch crops? Satellites hardly see this part.' },
]} />

<Term id="hazard">Hazard</Term>, <Term id="exposure">exposure</Term> and <Term id="vulnerability">vulnerability</Term> together make <Term id="disaster-risk">risk</Term>. This chapter only does the first part and some of the second: it scores hazards **on cropland**. The result is therefore a **hazard index on farmland**, not a risk map in the full sense. Naming it correctly is the first step to not over-reading it.

<Note kind="key">

A map labelled "risk" that is built from satellite data alone is almost always a hazard map, sometimes with exposure. Two provinces in the same red can carry very different risk if one has dykes, sluices and reserve fresh water and the other does not.

</Note>

## 14.2 Four indicators from earlier chapters

Each indicator is turned into a **score of 0–100** by a fixed rule, only on ~250 m cells with at least 50 % cropland in WorldCover 2021 (the same mask as the rice maps, Chapter 4). The rule that puts an indicator on the scale (<Term id="normalisation">normalisation</Term>) is the map maker's choice; the table states each one.

| Indicator | From | Score 0–100 | Available |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flooding | Chapter 8: monthly Sentinel-1 water share | Mekong: largest monthly water share in Jul–Dec 2018 (one of the large flood years). Red River: new water after typhoon Yagi, 12 Sep minus 31 Aug 2024 | both |
| Dry-season rain deficit | Chapter 9: Feb–Apr SPI-3 (CHIRPS, Funk et al., 2015; 5 km cells) | mean SPI-3 of 2016, 2020, 2024: 0 at ≥ 0, 100 at ≤ −2 | Mekong |
| Dry-season crop decline | Chapter 10: Feb–Apr EVI anomaly | share of 2016, 2020, 2024 with an anomaly ≤ −0.05 (0, 33, 67 or 100) | Mekong |
| Low-lying land | Chapter 11: DeltaDTM | 100 at ≤ 1 m, 0 at ≥ 3 m above EGM2008, linear in between | both |

The Red River Delta has only two indicators. The dry seasons of 2016, 2020 and 2024 were wetter than normal in the north (delta-mean Feb–Apr SPI-3 between +0.4 and +1.4, Chapter 9), so the rain-deficit score would be 0 everywhere. Spring EVI there is almost entirely hidden by cloud (Chapter 9).

On the 1,940 thousand ha of Mekong cropland, the four indicators look very different:

- **Flooding**: 26.9 % of the area scores 50 or more and 48.1 % scores 0. It is concentrated in the deeply flooded upper delta.
- **Crop decline**: 33.8 % of the area declined in at least two of the three dry seasons, and 35.2 % never did.
- **Low land**: 68.4 % scores 50 or more, meaning it lies below 2 m in DeltaDTM.
- **Rain deficit**: 99.5 % scores 50 or more, but scores only range from 52 to 72 (2 % to 98 % of cells). The whole delta was equally dry in those years.

## 14.3 Weighted overlay

The simplest way to combine indicators is a weighted mean, a form of <Term id="mcda">multi-criteria analysis</Term> widely used in GIS (Malczewski, 2006):

<Formula label="Composite hazard index of a cell" tex="R = \frac{\sum_k w_k\, s_k}{\sum_k w_k}, \qquad 0 \le s_k \le 100" where="
s_k :: score of indicator k in the cell (0–100)
w_k :: weight of indicator k; the sums only run over the indicators with data in that cell
R :: composite index, on the same 0–100 scale
" />

In each cell, the hazard with the largest <M tex="w_k s_k" /> is the cell's **dominant hazard**. A province is summarised by three numbers: the area-weighted mean index, the share of its area with R ≥ 50, and the hazard that dominates the most area.

With four equal weights, the mean index of Mekong cropland is 45.6 and 35.2 % of the area scores 50 or more. At the top are Hau Giang (52.6 % of its area high), Long An (48.6 %) and Kien Giang (43.9 %). At the bottom are Tra Vinh (6.4 %), Vinh Long (15.1 %) and Tien Giang (19.4 %). Low land dominates the most area (39.3 %), followed by rain deficit (26.0 %), crop decline (17.7 %) and flooding (17.0 %).

## 14.4 The weights decide the answer

No data say that floods are so many times "worse" than low land. A weight is a judgement: of experts, of farmers who were asked, or of policy. Three different sets show the consequences at once:

| Weights (flood / rain / crop / low) | Area with R ≥ 50 | Top three | Ben Tre | Tra Vinh |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 / 1 / 1 / 1 | 35.2 % | Hau Giang, Long An, Kien Giang | 23.2 % | 6.4 % |
| 3 / 1 / 1 / 1 | 28.5 % | Long An, Dong Thap, Kien Giang | 0.0 % | 0.4 % |
| 1 / 1 / 1 / 3 | 49.6 % | Hau Giang, Ca Mau, Bac Lieu | 49.5 % | 32.8 % |

When floods weigh most, the deeply flooded upper delta (Long An, Dong Thap) leads. When low land weighs most, the Ca Mau peninsula and the depressions between the Hau River and the Gulf of Thailand come first, and An Giang falls to the bottom (21.7 %). Same data, three different answers to "which province is most at risk".

<Note kind="warn">

Before publishing a multi-criteria map, try several weight sets (<Term id="sensitivity-analysis">sensitivity analysis</Term>) and report which provinces stay near the top whatever the weights. Conclusions that hold for only one set should say so (Saisana et al., 2005; OECD/JRC, 2008).

</Note>

## 14.6 Adaptation zoning and Resolution 120

<Term id="resolution-120">Resolution 120/NQ-CP</Term> of 17 November 2017 on sustainable and climate-resilient development of the Mekong Delta (Chapter 10) asks to respect natural laws and avoid rough intervention in nature, and to move from "living with floods" to "living proactively with floods, inundation, brackish and salt water". It calls for <Term id="ecological-subregion">ecological sub-regions</Term> (the flood plain, the freshwater zone, the brackish and saline zones) to guide development; for an agricultural structure built on three pillars, "aquaculture – fruit – rice", tied to those sub-regions; for room to drain floods; for brackish and salt water to be seen as a natural resource too; and it tasks the Ministry of Agriculture with reducing the area of rice and other crops that use much fresh water but have low commercial value (quotes translated from the Vietnamese text).

The dominant-hazard map shows part of the logic of that zoning, but only once the rain indicator is dropped. With equal weights, that nearly uniform indicator (about 61 points) wins wherever the other hazards are lower, so it paints the whole low upper delta orange. Set the rain weight to 0: flooding becomes the dominant hazard of Dong Thap, crop decline of An Giang and Long An, and low land dominates the other ten provinces, from Hau Giang and Kien Giang to the Ca Mau peninsula. It also shows the limits of the word "hazard". For Resolution 120, the flood in the flood plain is a hazard and also a source of silt and fish and a storage space to be kept. Salt water on the coast is what makes shrimp farming possible. A "higher is worse" index cannot express that.

<Note kind="why">

A multi-criteria map is most useful for **discussion**, not for **verdicts**. Put the parties (farmers, irrigation, planners) in front of the same map, let each set the weights the way they think, and look at where the map is always red and where it is red for only one set. That is how the FAO handbook (2017, section 6.3) links early warning to early action: information is only worth something when decision makers understand and trust it.

</Note>

<Depth level="advanced" title="Red River Delta: two indicators, little signal">

On 657 thousand ha of Red River cropland, the composite with two equal weights passes 50 on only 1.1 % of the area, most in Nam Dinh (5.3 %) and Ninh Binh (3.0 %). With low land weighted 3, Nam Dinh has 30.6 % of its area high and Thai Binh 16.2 %: the low coastal strip of the delta. A small result does not mean the Red River Delta is safe. Its main risks (typhoons, heavy rain, waterlogging inside the dykes, low river flow in the dry season) either cannot be seen through cloud by optical images or appear only once in the data.

</Depth>
